
### Know what Counts as an Incident

### Questions Leaders Should Ask

Executives and key staff should ask:

1. Do employees know what counts as reportable?
    
2. Is the reporting process simple?
    
3. Is there an emergency reporting path?
    
4. Are phishing, data exposure, account compromise, fraud attempts, and lost devices clearly covered?
    
5. Are employees trained to report mistakes quickly?
    
6. Are managers reinforcing reporting behavior?
    
7. Are reports tracked and reviewed for patterns?
    
8. Are contractors and external staff included?
    
9. Are legal, privacy, and insurance escalation points defined?
    
10. Does leadership model a no-blame reporting culture?
    

### Evidence Leaders Should Request

Useful evidence includes:

- Incident reporting procedure
    
- Employee reporting guide
    
- Phishing reporting process
    
- Lost/stolen device procedure
    
- Data exposure procedure
    
- Account compromise procedure
    
- Training completion records
    
- Incident and near-miss reports
    
- Escalation matrix
    
- Manager guidance
    
- Tabletop exercise results
    
- Post-incident review notes
    

### Short Scenario

An employee receives a suspicious invoice email, opens the attachment, realizes it looks strange, closes it, and continues working without reporting because nothing obvious happened.

Ask learners:

1. Is this reportable?
    
2. Why should it be reported?
    
3. What should the employee say in the report?
    
4. What should the employee avoid doing?
    

Expected answer:

Yes, it is reportable. The attachment may have contained malware or may indicate a wider phishing campaign. The employee should report what happened, when it happened, what was opened, and whether any credentials were entered. They should not delete the email, hide the mistake, or continue investigating alone.

### Know how to Report


### Manager Responsibilities

**Managers should:**

- Know the reporting channels themselves.
    
- Reinforce that fast reporting is expected.
    
- Escalate urgent reports immediately.
    
- Avoid blaming employees for honest mistakes.
    
- Make sure temporary staff and contractors know how to report.
    
- Support staff who must stop work during response.
    
- Never tell employees to hide, minimize, or delay reports.
    
- Ensure backup reporting routes are known if email or chat is unavailable.
    

A manager should not act as a bottleneck. If something may be urgent, it should go directly to the response channel.


### Short Scenario

An employee clicks a link in a suspicious email and enters their password. A few minutes later, they realize the website may have been fake.

Ask learners:

1. Which channel should they use?
    
2. What facts should they include?
    
3. What should they avoid doing?
    
4. Why is fast reporting important?
    

Expected answer:

They should report immediately using the approved incident channel. They should state that they clicked a link, entered their password, when it happened, what device and account were involved, and whether MFA was approved. They should not hide the mistake, delete evidence, or continue using the account for sensitive work unless instructed. Fast reporting may allow the company to reset credentials, revoke sessions, and prevent further compromise.

### Take the Correct First Actions


### Questions Leaders Should Ask

Executives and key staff should ask:

1. Do employees know the first four actions: stop, preserve, report, follow instructions?
    
2. Are first-response instructions simple enough to remember?
    
3. Are incident playbooks available for common scenarios?
    
4. Do managers know how to escalate suspected incidents?
    
5. Are employees trained not to delete evidence?
    
6. Are backup communication channels defined?
    
7. Are legal, privacy, insurance, and communications contacts included?
    
8. Are tabletop exercises used to test the process?
    
9. Are after-hours first-response instructions clear?
    
10. Are contractors and temporary staff included?



### Short Scenario

An employee clicks a suspicious link and enters their password. A few minutes later, they receive an MFA prompt they did not expect.

Ask learners:

1. What should the employee stop doing?
    
2. What evidence should they preserve?
    
3. What should they report?
    
4. What should they avoid doing?
    

Expected answer:

The employee should stop using the suspicious page, deny the unexpected MFA prompt, preserve the email or message, report immediately, and explain that they clicked the link and entered a password. They should not hide the mistake, approve MFA, delete evidence, or continue using the account for sensitive work unless instructed.

### Prepare and Exercise the Incident Response 


### Short Scenario

The company begins receiving reports from multiple employees that shared files are renamed and cannot be opened. One employee sees a ransom note. Email is still working, but the file server is not.

Ask learners:

1. Who should be notified first?
    
2. Who has authority to declare a major incident?
    
3. What systems or devices may need to be isolated?
    
4. Who contacts the IT provider or MSP?
    
5. Who checks backup status?
    
6. Who decides what employees should be told?
    
7. Who determines whether legal, insurance, or privacy escalation is needed?
    
8. What evidence should be preserved?
    
9. What business functions are most urgent to restore?
    
10. What would fail if the plan had never been exercised?
    

Expected answer:

The incident should be reported through the urgent channel and escalated to the incident lead. IT or the MSP should begin containment and evidence preservation. Leadership should activate decision-making, communication, legal/insurance review, and business continuity steps. Backup status should be verified before recovery decisions are made. The exercise should expose whether roles, contacts, authority, backup assumptions, and communication paths are ready.
### Questions Leaders Should Ask

Executives and key staff should ask:

1. When was the incident response plan last reviewed?
    
2. Who approved it?
    
3. Who is the incident lead?
    
4. Who has authority to make urgent business decisions?
    
5. Are after-hours contacts current?
    
6. Are legal, insurance, and communications contacts included?
    
7. Is our MSP or IT provider included in the plan?
    
8. What happens if email or chat is unavailable?
    
9. Which incident scenarios have we exercised?
    
10. When was the last tabletop exercise?
    
11. What gaps were found?
    
12. Were improvement actions completed?
    
13. Are backups and recovery connected to the plan?
    
14. Are managers trained on escalation?
    
15. Can employees find reporting instructions quickly?
    

### Evidence Leaders Should Request

Useful evidence includes:

- Current incident response plan
    
- Leadership approval record
    
- Incident response role list
    
- Escalation matrix
    
- Emergency contact list
    
- Employee reporting guide
    
- Incident playbooks
    
- Legal and insurance escalation procedure
    
- Communications plan
    
- Vendor and MSP contact list
    
- Backup and recovery test results
    
- Tabletop exercise agenda
    
- Tabletop exercise attendance
    
- Exercise findings
    
- After-action report
    
- Improvement action tracker
    
- Evidence that completed actions were verified
    
- Plan review history
    

Leadership should not accept “we have an incident response plan” as enough. The useful question is:

**When did we test it, what failed, and what did we fix?**


### Executives and key staff should ensure the company has:

- A written incident response plan
    
- Defined roles and decision-making authority
    
- Internal and external contact lists
    
- Legal, privacy, insurance, communications, and regulatory escalation procedures
    
- Procedures for involving managed service providers and other critical vendors
    
- Alternative communication methods
    
- A process for preserving evidence
    
- Regular tabletop exercises
    
- A process for capturing and implementing lessons learned
    

CISA’s small-business guidance explicitly states that leadership should review and approve the incident response plan before an incident occurs, and NIST’s current incident-response guidance treats response as part of broader cybersecurity risk management rather than an isolated technical activity. ([CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/cyber-guidance-small-businesses "Cyber Guidance for Small Businesses"))

### Ensure Backups and Redundancies are Rock Solid



### Short Scenario

A company suffers a ransomware incident. Shared files are encrypted, email still works, and employees cannot access the finance folder. The MSP says backups exist, but no one has tested a full restore in the past year.

Ask learners:

1. What should leadership ask first?
    
2. Why is “we have backups” not enough?
    
3. What recovery risks are present?
    
4. What redundancies may be needed while systems are restored?
    
5. What evidence should leadership request after recovery?
    

Expected answer:

Leadership should ask what systems are backed up, when the last clean backup was taken, whether backups are protected from ransomware, how long restore will take, and whether the backup has been tested. “We have backups” is not enough because backups may be incomplete, corrupted, inaccessible, too old, or too slow to restore. The business may need backup communication methods, manual finance processes, customer communication procedures, and clear recovery priorities. After recovery, leadership should request restore evidence, timeline results, gaps, and improvement actions.


### Evidence Leaders Should Request

Useful evidence includes:

- Critical system inventory
    
- Data inventory
    
- Backup coverage report
    
- Backup schedule
    
- Backup success and failure report
    
- Restore test results
    
- Recovery time results
    
- Recovery point results
    
- Backup access control list
    
- MFA status for backup administration
    
- Offline or immutable backup configuration evidence
    
- Backup encryption evidence
    
- Backup deletion protection settings
    
- SaaS backup coverage review
    
- MSP or provider responsibility matrix
    
- Recovery runbooks
    
- Disaster recovery plan
    
- Business continuity plan
    
- Emergency contact list
    
- Manual fallback procedures
    
- Backup exception register
    
- Post-test improvement tracker
    

Leadership should not accept “backups are running” as sufficient evidence.

The stronger evidence is:

**We restored the right system, from a protected backup, within the required time, and verified the data worked.**



### Warning Signs of Weak Backup and Redundancy

The company may have weak backup readiness if:

- Nobody knows what is backed up
    
- Backups are managed by only one person
    
- Restore tests have never been performed
    
- Backup failures are not monitored
    
- Critical SaaS systems are assumed to be recoverable
    
- Backups use the same credentials as production systems
    
- Backups can be deleted by compromised administrators
    
- No offline or immutable backup exists
    
- No one knows recovery time expectations
    
- No one knows how much data could be lost
    
- Recovery documentation is stored only in the systems that may be down
    
- Contact lists are outdated
    
- Manual fallback processes do not exist
    
- Executives have never reviewed recovery capability
    
- The MSP says “we have it covered” but provides no test evidence
    

These are business continuity problems, not just technical gaps.



### Executives should not ask only, “Do we have backups?” That question is too weak.

They should ask:

- What systems and data are backed up?
    
- How frequently are backups taken?
    
- Are critical backups protected from ransomware?
    
- Are offline, isolated, or otherwise protected copies maintained?
    
- Who can delete or alter backups?
    
- When was the last successful restoration test?
    
- How long would recovery take?
    
- How much data could the company afford to lose?
    
- Which business operations must be restored first?
    
- Can the company operate if email, cloud services, or core systems are unavailable?
    

CISA recommends regular automated backups, protections for backups, and backup plans aligned with the organization’s recovery objectives. ([CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/cyber-essentials "Cyber Essentials"))

**Evidence to request:** approved incident response plans, exercise reports, call trees, backup coverage reports, restoration-test results, recovery objectives, and post-incident improvement records.
