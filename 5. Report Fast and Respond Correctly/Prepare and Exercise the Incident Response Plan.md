### Goal

This incident response plan section is robust because it is the executive-control center of procedures to enact in case of issues both before and when they occur. 

Incident response is a key part of an organizations broader cyber risk management spread across preparation, detection, response, and recovery.

### Learning Objective

By the end of this section, learners should understand why every company needs a written and comprehensive incident response plan, what that plan should cover, and why it must be tested before a real incident happens.

Employees should learn this core behavior:

**Know your role in reporting and first response. Do not improvise during an incident.**

Managers and executives should learn this assurance principle:

**The company must have a written, approved, tested, and regularly updated incident response plan.**

### Why This Matters

A cyber incident is a bad time to decide who is in charge, who calls the IT provider, who contacts legal counsel, who talks to customers, whether backups work, or whether the company should shut down a system.

Without a plan, the company may lose time arguing, guessing, or waiting for the wrong person to approve the right action.

**A weak response can lead to:**

- Longer downtime
    
- More data loss
    
- Confused decision-making
    
- Missed legal or regulatory obligations
    
- Poor customer communication
    
- Evidence being destroyed
    
- Delayed containment
    
- Larger recovery costs
    
- Reputational damage
    
- Repeated incidents from the same root cause
    

The point of an incident response plan is not to predict every possible attack. The point is to make sure the company knows how to coordinate quickly when something goes wrong.

### Core Rule

An incident response plan should be:

**Written, assigned, approved, accessible, tested, and improved.**

A plan that sits in a folder and is never exercised is not enough.

The stronger standard is:

**Could the company use this plan under pressure, outside normal hours, when systems may be unavailable?**

### What the Incident Response Plan Should Include

A practical SME incident response plan should include the following elements.

#### 1. Clear Incident Definition

The plan should define what counts as a reportable incident or suspected incident.

**Examples:**

- Phishing
    
- Account compromise
    
- Malware
    
- Ransomware
    
- Data exposure
    
- Lost or stolen devices
    
- Business email compromise
    
- Unauthorized access
    
- Suspicious payment or vendor-change requests
    
- Unapproved tool or AI data exposure
    
- System outage caused by suspected cyber activity
    

Employees should not need to guess whether something is worth reporting.

#### 2. Roles and Responsibilities

The plan should define who does what.

**Common roles include:**

- Incident lead
    
- Executive decision-maker
    
- IT or security lead
    
- Managed service provider contact
    
- Legal counsel
    
- Privacy or compliance lead
    
- HR representative
    
- Communications lead
    
- Finance representative
    
- Business operations lead
    
- Vendor or supplier contact owner
    
- Cyber insurance contact, if applicable
    

For SMEs, one person may hold more than one role. That is acceptable. What is not acceptable is having no assigned role.

#### 3. Escalation and Decision Authority

The plan should define who can make urgent decisions.

**Examples:**

- Who can disconnect a system?
    
- Who can disable accounts?
    
- Who can authorize emergency spending?
    
- Who can contact external incident response support?
    
- Who can approve customer notifications?
    
- Who can contact law enforcement?
    
- Who can approve public statements?
    
- Who can decide whether to pay for emergency recovery services?
    
- Who can activate the business continuity plan?
    

Decision authority must be clear before the incident.

#### 4. Reporting Channels

The plan should identify how employees report incidents.

**Include:**

- Phishing report button
    
- Security email address
    
- Service desk portal
    
- Emergency phone number
    
- Manager escalation route
    
- After-hours contact path
    
- Backup communication channel if email or chat is unavailable
    

Do not rely only on email. In some incidents, email may be unavailable or compromised.

#### 5. Contact List

The plan should include current contact details for key people and providers.

**Include:**

- Internal response team
    
- Executives
    
- IT provider or MSP
    
- Cybersecurity provider
    
- Legal counsel
    
- Insurance broker or cyber insurer
    
- Communications or PR support
    
- Key software vendors
    
- Cloud providers
    
- Critical suppliers
    
- Law enforcement or government reporting contacts, where applicable
    

This list should be reviewed regularly. An outdated contact list breaks the plan.

#### 6. Incident Severity Levels

The plan should define severity levels so the company knows how urgently to respond.

Example model:

- **Low:** Suspicious email reported, no click, no data exposure known.  
- **Medium:** Employee clicked a suspicious link or opened an attachment.  
- **High:** Credentials entered, device compromise suspected, sensitive data exposed.  
- **Critical:** Ransomware, major outage, confirmed unauthorized access, large data exposure, executive account compromise, or active fraud.

Severity levels should trigger different escalation paths.

#### 7. Response Playbooks

The plan should include short playbooks for common incidents.

**Useful playbooks include:**

- Phishing
    
- Account compromise
    
- Ransomware
    
- Lost or stolen device
    
- Data sent to the wrong recipient
    
- Business email compromise
    
- Vendor bank-detail fraud
    
- Malware warning
    
- Unapproved AI or tool data exposure
    
- Cloud folder shared publicly
    
- Insider or unauthorized access concern
    

Each playbook should say what to do first, who to contact, what evidence to preserve, and what decisions may be needed.

#### 8. Evidence Preservation

The plan should explain how to preserve useful evidence.

**Evidence may include:**

- Emails
    
- Attachments
    
- Links
    
- Chat messages
    
- Login alerts
    
- Security warnings
    
- Screenshots
    
- Device names
    
- Account names
    
- File names
    
- Access logs
    
- Timeline of actions
    
- Details of what was clicked, opened, entered, approved, or shared
    

Employees should not delete messages, wipe devices, or alter files unless instructed.

#### 9. Communication Rules

The plan should define who communicates internally and externally.

Communication may be needed with:

- Employees
    
- Executives
    
- Customers
    
- Vendors
    
- Regulators
    
- Insurers
    
- Law enforcement
    
- Media
    
- Board members
    
- Business partners
    

Employees should know not to make unauthorized statements, contact suspected attackers, post publicly, or notify customers unless instructed.

Poor communication can create legal, reputational, and operational problems.

#### 10. Legal, Privacy, and Insurance Escalation

Some incidents may trigger legal, contractual, privacy, regulatory, or insurance obligations.

**The plan should define when to involve:**

- Legal counsel
    
- Privacy officer or responsible manager
    
- Cyber insurance provider
    
- External incident response provider
    
- Regulators, where required
    
- Contract owners
    
- Customer relationship owners
    

Do not wait until after the incident to find out whether notice deadlines or insurance conditions apply.

#### 11. Vendor and MSP Coordination

Many SMEs rely on external providers for IT, cloud systems, backups, email, security tools, websites, finance systems, or HR platforms.

**The plan should identify:**

- Which providers support critical systems
    
- Who contacts them during an incident
    
- What support hours they provide
    
- What emergency escalation is available
    
- What evidence or logs they can provide
    
- Whether they are included in exercises
    
- What contractual response commitments exist
    

If a provider is critical to recovery, they must be part of response planning.

#### 12. Backup and Recovery Connection

The incident response plan must connect to backup and recovery plans.

**The company should know:**

- What systems are backed up
    
- How often backups run
    
- Who can restore backups
    
- How long restoration may take
    
- Whether backups are protected from ransomware
    
- When backups were last tested
    
- Which systems must be restored first
    
- How the business operates during downtime
    

Incident response without recovery planning is incomplete.

### What Employees Should Know

Employees do not need to memorize the entire incident response plan.

**They should know:**

1. What to report
    
2. How to report
    
3. What first actions to take
    
4. What not to do
    
5. Who gives instructions during an incident
    
6. Where to find the quick-reference guide
    
7. How to report if normal systems are unavailable
    

Employee training should be simple and repeated.

### What Managers Should Know

**Managers should know:**

- How to escalate suspected incidents
    
- When to stop normal work
    
- How to support affected employees
    
- How to preserve evidence
    
- How to avoid unauthorized communication
    
- How to keep teams calm and factual
    
- How to coordinate with IT, security, HR, legal, or leadership
    
- How to avoid blaming employees who report quickly
    

Managers are often the first escalation point. If they hesitate, the incident slows down.

### What Executives Must Own

Executives do not need to perform technical response tasks, but they do own the business response.

**Executives should ensure:**

- The plan exists
    
- The plan is approved
    
- Roles are assigned
    
- Critical providers are included
    
- Legal and insurance contacts are defined
    
- Backups are tested
    
- Communication authority is clear
    
- The plan is exercised
    
- Lessons learned are implemented
    
- Funding and support are available
    

An incident response plan without executive ownership is usually weak.

### Exercising the Plan

A plan must be tested before a real incident.

The most practical method for SMEs is a tabletop exercise.

A tabletop exercise is a discussion-based scenario where participants walk through how they would respond to a realistic incident.

The goal is not to embarrass anyone. The goal is to find gaps before attackers do.

### Tabletop Exercise Goals

**A good exercise should test whether:**

- People know their roles
    
- Contact details are current
    
- Reporting channels work
    
- Escalation is clear
    
- Leadership can make timely decisions
    
- IT or the MSP knows what to do
    
- Backups and recovery assumptions are realistic
    
- Legal, insurance, and communication steps are understood
    
- Evidence is preserved
    
- Customer or vendor communication is controlled
    

The exercise should reveal weaknesses. That is the point.

### Tabletop Exercise Scenarios

Useful scenarios for testing the incident response plan include:

#### Scenario 1: Business Email Compromise

A finance employee receives what appears to be a vendor bank-detail change. Payment is made before the fraud is detected.

**Test:**

- Payment escalation
    
- Vendor verification
    
- Bank contact process
    
- Legal and insurance escalation
    
- Executive communication
    
- Evidence preservation
    

#### Scenario 2: Ransomware

Several employees report locked files and ransom notes. Critical shared folders are unavailable.

**Test:**

- Device isolation
    
- Backup restoration
    
- Communication when systems are down
    
- Executive decision-making
    
- MSP coordination
    
- Business continuity
    

#### Scenario 3: Compromised Executive Account

Employees receive strange messages from a senior executive’s email account requesting sensitive documents.

**Test:**

- Account containment
    
- MFA reset
    
- Internal warning
    
- Executive communication
    
- Data exposure review
    
- Investigation steps
    

#### Scenario 4: Data Sent to Wrong Recipient

An employee sends a spreadsheet containing customer and employee information to an external recipient by mistake.

**Test:**

- Reporting
    
- Data classification
    
- Legal/privacy escalation
    
- Customer notification decision
    
- Evidence and timeline collection
    

#### Scenario 5: Lost Laptop During Travel

An employee loses a company laptop at an airport.

**Test:**

- Lost-device reporting
    
- Remote lock or wipe
    
- Encryption status
    
- Data exposure assessment
    
- Travel policy
    
- Replacement process
    

#### Scenario 6: Unapproved AI Tool Exposure

An employee uploads a confidential contract and customer file to an unapproved AI tool.

**Test:**

- AI use policy
    
- Data exposure triage
    
- Vendor/tool assessment
    
- Legal review
    
- Employee reporting
    
- Remediation steps
    

### How to Run a Tabletop Exercise

**A simple tabletop exercise can follow this format:**

1. Choose one realistic scenario.
    
2. Invite the right participants.
    
3. Assign a facilitator.
    
4. Present the initial facts.
    
5. Ask participants what they would do.
    
6. Add new developments during the discussion.
    
7. Record decisions, gaps, and questions.
    
8. Identify what worked and what failed.
    
9. Assign improvement actions.
    
10. Update the plan.
    

The exercise should test the actual plan, not theoretical best practice.

### Who Should Participate

Participants should include the people who would be involved in a real incident.

**Depending on the scenario, include:**

- Executive sponsor
    
- Incident lead
    
- IT or security lead
    
- Managed service provider
    
- Legal counsel
    
- HR
    
- Finance
    
- Operations
    
- Communications
    
- Customer relationship owner
    
- Data owner
    
- Department manager
    
- Cyber insurance contact, if appropriate
    

Do not make tabletop exercises IT-only. Cyber incidents create business decisions.

### Exercise Frequency

At minimum, SMEs should run an incident response tabletop exercise annually.

**More frequent exercises may be needed after:**

- Major system changes
    
- New IT provider or MSP
    
- New executive team
    
- New cyber insurance requirements
    
- Major business growth
    
- Significant security incident
    
- New cloud, AI, or remote work tools
    
- Changes in legal or regulatory obligations
    

Shorter department-specific drills can also be useful for finance, HR, executives, and operations.

### What to Measure

**After an exercise, the company should be able to answer:**

- Did everyone know their role?
    
- Were contact details current?
    
- Was escalation clear?
    
- Were decisions made quickly?
    
- Were legal and insurance steps understood?
    
- Were backup and recovery assumptions realistic?
    
- Were communication rules clear?
    
- Were external providers reachable?
    
- Were evidence requirements understood?
    
- What gaps were found?
    
- Who owns each improvement?
    
- By when will improvements be completed?
    

A tabletop exercise without follow-up is just a meeting.

### After-Action Review

Every real incident and every exercise should end with an after-action review.

**The review should identify:**

- What happened
    
- What worked
    
- What did not work
    
- What decisions were delayed
    
- What information was missing
    
- What communication failed
    
- What technical controls failed
    
- What process changes are needed
    
- What training is needed
    
- What plan updates are required
    
- Who owns each action
    
- When each action is due
    

The goal is improvement, not blame.

### What Employees Should Do

**Employees should:**

1. Know where the incident reporting guide is.
    
2. Report incidents quickly.
    
3. Follow first-response instructions.
    
4. Preserve evidence.
    
5. Follow instructions from the response team.
    
6. Participate in exercises if asked.
    
7. Be honest about mistakes.
    
8. Avoid unauthorized communication.
    
9. Use backup reporting channels if normal systems are unavailable.
    
10. Learn from incident reminders and post-exercise lessons.
    

### What Employees Should Not Do

**Employees should not:**

- Improvise during an incident
    
- Contact suspected attackers
    
- Delete suspicious messages or files
    
- Discuss incidents publicly
    
- Notify customers or vendors unless authorized
    
- Ignore incident instructions
    
- Continue using compromised systems
    
- Hide mistakes
    
- Assume someone else is handling it
    
- Treat exercises as box-ticking
    

### Manager Responsibilities

**Managers should:**

- Know the incident reporting and escalation process.
    
- Make sure their teams know how to report.
    
- Support employees who report quickly.
    
- Escalate suspected incidents without delay.
    
- Participate in tabletop exercises.
    
- Identify department-specific risks.
    
- Keep staff calm and factual during incidents.
    
- Prevent unauthorized external communication.
    
- Help collect business-impact information.
    
- Track improvement actions assigned to their department.
    

Managers should not slow down reporting by trying to privately assess everything first.

### Executive and Key Staff Assurance Points

Executives and key staff should verify that the incident response plan is operational.

**Leadership should ensure:**

- The plan is written and current
    
- The plan is approved by leadership
    
- Roles and authority are assigned
    
- Contact lists are current
    
- External providers are included
    
- Legal, privacy, and insurance steps are defined
    
- Backup and recovery assumptions are tested
    
- Employees know how to report
    
- Managers know how to escalate
    
- Tabletop exercises are performed
    
- Lessons learned are tracked to completion
    
- The plan is updated after incidents, exercises, or major changes
    

The leadership question is:

**Can the company execute the plan under pressure, or does the plan only look good on paper?**

### Warning Signs of a Weak Incident Response Plan

The company may not be ready if:

- The plan has not been reviewed in over a year
    
- Only IT knows the plan exists
    
- Contact lists are outdated
    
- No one knows who has decision authority
    
- Legal, insurance, and communications are missing
    
- The MSP is not included
    
- Employees do not know how to report
    
- There is no after-hours process
    
- Backups are assumed but not tested
    
- No tabletop exercises have been run
    
- Exercises happen but no improvements are tracked
    
- Managers are unsure how to escalate
    
- Executives are not involved
    
- The plan is too long or too vague to use under pressure
    

A plan that cannot be used quickly is not a response plan. It is documentation.


### Key Takeaway

Incident response must be prepared before the incident.

Employees need simple reporting and first-response instructions. Managers need escalation clarity. Executives need decision authority, legal and communication readiness, provider coordination, backup confidence, and tested playbooks.

A plan is only useful if people can execute it.

**Do not wait for a real incident to discover that no one knows what to do.**
