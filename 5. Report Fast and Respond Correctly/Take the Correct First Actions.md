### Goal

The employee-level rule should be simple: **stop, preserve, report, follow instructions**. The objective for staff is to stop the risky activity, avoid further interaction, report immediately, preserve evidence, and follow instructions.

At an organization level - general incident response guidance emphasizes preparation, detection, response, and recovery activities. Within incident playbooks, the security team will work through steps such as incident declaration, evidence handling, containment, and coordinated escalation as structured response activities.

### Learning Objective

By the end of this section, we will know what to do in the first few minutes after a suspected cybersecurity incident.

Employees should learn this core behavior:

**Stop risky activity, preserve evidence, report immediately, and follow instructions.**

Managers and executives should learn this assurance principle:

**The company must define clear first-response actions so employees do not accidentally make an incident worse.**

### Why This Matters

The first few minutes matter.

When something suspicious happens, employees may panic, continue clicking, delete evidence, try to fix the issue themselves, or wait too long to report. These actions can make the incident harder to contain and investigate.

**Correct first actions can help the company:**

- Preserve evidence
    
- Stop additional exposure
    
- Protect accounts
    
- Contain malware
    
- Reduce data loss
    
- Warn other employees
    
- Restore systems faster
    
- Escalate legal, privacy, or insurance issues when needed
    

The goal is not for employees to become incident responders. The goal is for employees to avoid making the situation worse.

### Core Rule

When a suspected incident happens:

**Stop. Preserve. Report. Follow instructions.**

Do not investigate alone. Do not delete evidence. Do not keep interacting with suspicious content.

### Step 1: Stop the Risky Activity

Employees should stop the action that may be causing harm.

**Examples:**

- Stop clicking links.
    
- Stop opening attachments.
    
- Stop entering passwords.
    
- Stop approving MFA prompts.
    
- Stop replying to the suspicious sender.
    
- Stop using a potentially compromised account for sensitive work.
    
- Stop sharing a file if the wrong access was granted.
    
- Stop working on a device showing serious warnings.
    

Stopping does not mean trying to fix everything. It means preventing further risky action.

### Step 2: Preserve Evidence

Evidence helps the response team understand what happened.

**Employees should preserve:**

- Suspicious emails
    
- Links
    
- Attachments
    
- Chat messages
    
- Screenshots of warnings
    
- Error messages
    
- File names
    
- Sender information
    
- Time of the event
    
- Device or account involved
    
- Details of what was clicked, opened, entered, approved, or shared
    

Employees should not delete suspicious messages or files before reporting unless instructed.

### Step 3: Report Immediately

Employees should report through the approved company channel.

**The report should include:**

- What happened
    
- When it happened
    
- What device, account, file, or system was involved
    
- Whether anything was clicked, opened, downloaded, entered, approved, or shared
    
- Whether sensitive data may be involved
    
- Whether the issue is still happening
    

A fast, honest report is more valuable than a perfect report.



### Step 4: Follow Instructions

After reporting, employees should follow instructions from IT, security, management, legal, HR, or the incident response lead.

**They may be asked to:**

- Stop using a device
    
- Keep the device powered on
    
- Disconnect from a network
    
- Change a password through the official process
    
- Deny further MFA prompts
    
- Forward or submit a suspicious email through the approved method
    
- Provide screenshots or details
    
- Avoid contacting external parties
    
- Use a backup communication channel
    
- Wait before deleting or modifying files
    

The right action depends on the incident type. That is why employees should follow the response team’s instructions rather than improvise.

### Key: Report Mistakes Without Delay or Shame

If you clicked a suspicious link, opened an attachment, entered a password, approved an MFA prompt, sent data to the wrong person, or used an unapproved tool, report it immediately.

Do not hide the mistake, wait to see what happens, or try to fix it quietly. The company can respond faster when it knows the truth early. A quick report can help reset credentials, block malicious links, remove phishing emails, lock devices, revoke access, or contain exposed data.

Reporting a mistake is responsible behavior. Delaying or hiding it creates greater risk.

**Employees should be told clearly:**

**You will not be blamed for reporting quickly. The bigger problem is silence.**

### What Employees Should Do

**Employees should:**

1. Stop the risky action.
    
2. Preserve the message, file, device, or alert.
    
3. Report immediately.
    
4. Be honest about what happened.
    
5. Include whether they clicked, opened, entered, approved, or shared anything.
    
6. Keep the device or account available for review.
    
7. Follow instructions from the response team.
    
8. Ask for clarification if instructions are unclear.
    
9. Report new developments if the situation changes.
    
10. Stay available for follow-up questions.
    

### What Employees Should Not Do

**Employees should not:**

- Hide the incident
    
- Delete suspicious emails before reporting
    
- Wipe or reset a device without instruction
    
- Install cleanup tools from the internet
    
- Keep clicking to investigate
    
- Forward suspicious files to coworkers
    
- Contact the suspected attacker
    
- Approve unexpected MFA prompts
    
- Change settings randomly
    
- Post about the incident externally
    
- Tell customers, vendors, or the public unless authorized
    
- Use a potentially compromised account for sensitive communication
    

The wrong first action can destroy evidence or increase damage.

### First Actions by Incident Type

#### Suspicious Email

**Do:**

- Use the phishing report button or approved reporting method.
    
- Leave the email available for review.
    
- Report whether you clicked, opened, replied, or entered information.
    

**Do not:**

- Forward it to coworkers.
    
- Delete it before reporting.
    
- Click additional links to test it.
    

#### Clicked Link or Entered Credentials

**Do:**

- Report immediately.
    
- Say exactly what was entered.
    
- Stop using the account for sensitive work until instructed.
    

**Do not:**

- Hide the mistake.
    
- Keep trying the link.
    
- Change passwords through the suspicious page.
    

#### Unexpected MFA Prompt

**Do:**

- Deny the prompt.
    
- Report it.
    
- Note the time and system shown.
    

**Do not:**

- Approve it to make it stop.
    
- Share MFA codes with anyone.
    

#### Lost or Stolen Device

**Do:**

- Report immediately using the urgent channel.
    
- Provide device type, location, and time.
    
- Say whether company data may be accessible.
    

**Do not:**

- Wait to see if it turns up.
    
- Assume the device is safe because it has a password.
    

#### Data Sent to Wrong Person

**Do:**

- Report immediately.
    
- Identify what was sent, to whom, and when.
    
- Follow instructions on containment.
    

**Do not:**

- Try to quietly handle it yourself.
    
- Ask the recipient to delete it without informing the company.
    

#### Strange Device Behavior

**Do:**

- Stop sensitive work.
    
- Report warnings, pop-ups, file changes, or redirects.
    
- Follow IT/security instructions.
    

**Do not:**

- Download cleanup software.
    
- Delete suspicious files.
    
- Move files to shared drives.
    

### Manager Responsibilities

**Managers should:**

- Reinforce the stop, preserve, report, follow instructions rule.
    
- Avoid blaming employees for honest mistakes.
    
- Escalate reports quickly.
    
- Support employees who need to pause work during response.
    
- Avoid giving technical instructions unless they are part of the approved process.
    
- Ensure contractors and temporary staff know first-response actions.
    
- Prevent unofficial communication about the incident.
    

Managers should not tell employees to “just delete it,” “just restart,” or “keep working” when an incident may be active.

### Executive and Key Staff Assurance Points

Executives and key staff should verify that first-response actions are defined before an incident occurs.

**Leadership should ensure the company has:**

- Clear employee first-response instructions
    
- Incident reporting channels
    
- Role-based response playbooks
    
- Escalation paths
    
- Evidence preservation guidance
    
- Lost/stolen device procedures
    
- Account compromise procedures
    
- Data exposure procedures
    
- Malware response procedures
    
- Backup communication methods
    
- Legal, privacy, insurance, and communications escalation points
    

The leadership question is:

**Do employees know what to do in the first five minutes, and do managers know how to escalate without delay?**


### Evidence Leaders Should Request

Useful evidence includes:

- Employee incident quick-reference guide
    
- Incident response plan
    
- Incident playbooks
    
- Escalation matrix
    
- Evidence preservation guidance
    
- Phishing response process
    
- Account compromise procedure
    
- Lost/stolen device procedure
    
- Data exposure procedure
    
- Malware response procedure
    
- Tabletop exercise results
    
- Training completion records
    
- Post-incident review notes
    

### Key Takeaway

The first response does not need to be technical. It needs to be correct.

Employees should stop risky activity, preserve evidence, report immediately, and follow instructions. Managers should escalate quickly without blame. Executives should verify that first-response guidance, playbooks, escalation paths, and backup communication methods are defined and tested.

**The first few minutes can reduce damage — or make it worse.**
