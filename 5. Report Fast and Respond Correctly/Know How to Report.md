### Goal

Ensure at your company that all staff are provided with and know how to use all the actual reporting channels, such as phishing-report buttons, security team email addresses, service desk contacts, emergency phone numbers, manager escalation routes and backup communication methods.

### Learning Objective

By the end of this section, we will understand the probable procedures in place at your organization to report a suspected cybersecurity incident, what information to include, and what to avoid doing while the report is being handled.

Employees should learn this core behavior:

**Use the approved reporting channel immediately and provide clear facts.**

Managers and executives should learn this assurance principle:

**The company must make incident reporting simple, visible, fast, and reliable.**

### Why This Matters

Knowing what counts as an incident is not enough. Employees also need to know where to report it, how fast to report it, and what details to provide.

A delayed or vague report can slow down containment. A clear report can help the response team act quickly.

**Fast reporting helps the company:**

- Block malicious links
    
- Remove phishing emails from other inboxes
    
- Reset compromised passwords
    
- Revoke suspicious sessions
    
- Lock or wipe lost devices
    
- Investigate malware warnings
    
- Contain data exposure
    
- Escalate legal, privacy, or insurance issues
    
- Warn other employees
    

The key is to provide secure channels for reporting incidents, phishing attempts, malware, and vulnerabilities.
### Core Rule

Employees should not spend time deciding whether the issue is “serious enough.”

The rule is:

**Report quickly through the approved channel. Include facts. Follow instructions.**

A weak report says:

“Something weird happened.”

A stronger report says:

“I opened an attachment from an unexpected invoice email at 10:15 AM. A login page appeared. I did not enter my password. The email is still in my inbox.”

### Approved Reporting Channels

Each company should define its exact reporting channels. Within your organization ensure you have the exact contact details in place and keep them updated.

**Common reporting channels include:**

- Phishing report button
    
- Security email address
    
- IT help desk portal
    
- Service desk phone number
    
- Emergency incident hotline
    
- Manager escalation path
    
- Internal chat channel for urgent IT/security issues
    
- Managed service provider contact
    
- Backup communication method if email or chat is unavailable
    

Employees should know which channel to use for normal reports and which channel to use for urgent incidents.

### What to Report

**Employees should report:**

- Suspicious emails, texts, calls, or QR codes
    
- Clicked links or opened attachments
    
- Entered credentials on suspicious pages
    
- Unexpected MFA prompts
    
- Lost or stolen devices
    
- Security warnings
    
- Unusual device behavior
    
- Data sent to the wrong person
    
- Public links or excessive sharing
    
- Suspicious payment, payroll, or vendor requests
    
- Unauthorized account access
    
- Unapproved tool or AI data exposure
    
- Messages sent from their account without permission
    

If unsure, report anyway.

### What Details to Include

**A good incident report should include:**

1. What happened
    
2. When it happened
    
3. Which device, account, system, or file was involved
    
4. Who sent or received the message or data
    
5. What action was taken
    
6. Whether anything was clicked, opened, downloaded, entered, approved, or shared
    
7. What sensitive data may be involved
    
8. Whether the issue is still happening
    
9. Any screenshots, messages, alerts, or files if safe and requested
    
10. Your contact details for follow-up
    

Employees do not need to use technical language. Clear facts are better than guesses.

### What Not to Do While Reporting

**Employees should not:**

- Delete suspicious emails before reporting
    
- Forward suspicious files to coworkers
    
- Keep clicking to investigate
    
- Try to clean malware themselves
    
- Install online cleanup tools
    
- Reset or wipe the device without instruction
    
- Approve unexpected MFA prompts
    
- Continue using a possibly compromised account for sensitive work
    
- Contact the suspected attacker
    
- Post about the incident publicly
    
- Hide mistakes or delay because of embarrassment
    

The response team needs evidence. Do not destroy or alter it unless instructed.

### Reporting by Incident Type

#### Suspicious Email or Message

**Best action:**

Use the phishing report button or approved reporting channel.

**Include:**

- Sender
    
- Subject
    
- Time received
    
- Whether you clicked, opened, replied, or entered information
    

#### Clicked Link or Opened Attachment

**Best action:**

Report immediately.

**Include:**

- What was clicked or opened
    
- Whether a file downloaded
    
- Whether credentials were entered
    
- Whether MFA was approved
    
- Whether the device changed behavior afterward
    

#### Unexpected MFA Prompt

**Best action:**

Deny the prompt and report.

**Include:**

- Time of prompt
    
- System or app shown
    
- Location or device shown, if visible
    
- Whether repeated prompts occurred
    

#### Data Sent to Wrong Person

**Best action:**

Report immediately.

**Include:**

- What was sent
    
- Who received it
    
- When it was sent
    
- What type of data was included
    
- Whether the recipient is internal or external
    

#### Lost or Stolen Device

**Best action:**

Report immediately through the urgent channel.

**Include:**

- Device type
    
- Approximate time and location
    
- Whether it was locked
    
- What systems or data may be accessible
    
- Whether theft is suspected
    

#### Unusual Device Behavior

**Best action:**

Stop sensitive work and report.

**Include:**

- Warning messages
    
- Strange pop-ups
    
- Browser redirects
    
- Missing or changed files
    
- What happened before the issue started
    


### Executive and Key Staff Assurance Points

Executives and key staff should verify that incident reporting works in practice.

**Leadership should ensure the company has:**

- Clear reporting channels
    
- A visible employee reporting guide
    
- An emergency escalation process
    
- A phishing reporting method
    
- A lost-device reporting process
    
- A data exposure reporting process
    
- A backup communication method
    
- A triage and escalation workflow
    
- Defined roles for IT, security, legal, HR, communications, and management
    
- Training for employees and managers
    
- Testing through tabletop exercises or simulations
    

The leadership question is:

**Can any employee quickly report a cyber incident, even outside normal business hours or if email is unavailable?**

### Questions Leaders Should Ask

**Executives and key staff should ask:**

1. Do employees know where to report suspicious activity?
    
2. Is there one simple reporting path?
    
3. Is there an urgent path for serious incidents?
    
4. What happens after a report is submitted?
    
5. Who receives reports?
    
6. Who triages them?
    
7. Are reports tracked?
    
8. Are after-hours incidents covered?
    
9. Is there a backup channel if email or chat is down?
    
10. Are managers trained not to block or delay reporting?
    
11. Are contractors and temporary staff included?
    
12. Are reporting drills or tabletop exercises performed?
    

### Evidence Leaders Should Request

Useful evidence includes:

- Incident reporting procedure
    
- Employee quick-reference guide
    
- Phishing reporting workflow
    
- Help desk escalation workflow
    
- Emergency contact list
    
- After-hours response procedure
    
- Lost-device reporting procedure
    
- Data exposure reporting procedure
    
- Incident tracking records
    
- Training completion records
    
- Tabletop exercise results
    
- Post-incident review notes
    

### Key Takeaway

Incident reporting should be simple, fast, and factual.

Employees should use the approved reporting channel, include what happened, and avoid deleting evidence or investigating alone. Managers should escalate without blame or delay. Executives should verify that reporting channels, escalation paths, after-hours coverage, and backup communication methods actually work.

**A good report gives the response team time to protect the company.**
