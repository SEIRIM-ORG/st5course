### Goals

Here we make one point clearly: unapproved tools are not just “productivity shortcuts”; they are data-sharing and access-control risks.

Here we cover topics like personal email, personal cloud storage, unapproved applications, browser extensions, file-transfer tools, third-party integrations, and generative AI. Employees should understand that placing company information into an external tool may be a form of data disclosure.

### Learning Objective

By the end of this section, learners will understand why company information should only be used in approved tools, cloud platforms, applications, browser extensions, file-sharing services, and AI systems.

Employees should learn this core behavior:

**Use company information only in approved tools and approved AI services. If a tool is not approved, do not upload, paste, connect, sync, or store company data in it.**

Managers and executives should learn this assurance principle:

**The company must know which tools are being used, what data they access, who approved them, and whether they are safe for the type of information being handled.**

### Why This Matters

Most employees use many digital tools every day: email, chat, cloud storage, spreadsheets, project management apps, CRM systems, file-transfer tools, browser extensions, note-taking apps, design tools, and AI assistants.

These tools can improve productivity. They can also create serious risk when they are used without approval and oversight.

**An unapproved tool may:**

- Store company data outside approved systems
    
- Copy files to unknown locations
    
- Train or improve models using user-provided content
    
- Retain prompts, uploads, or chat history
    
- Allow external access without company visibility
    
- Lack required security controls
    
- Lack contractual protection
    
- Be outside company backup, retention, and deletion processes
    
- Connect to email, calendars, contacts, or cloud files
    
- Be compromised or malicious
    
- Make incident response harder because IT may not know the tool exists
    

This is often called **shadow IT** or, in the case of artificial intelligence, **shadow AI**.

The core problem is simple:

**If the company does not know a tool is being used, it cannot properly protect the data inside it.**

### Core Rule

Before using any tool with company information, ask:

**Is this tool approved for this type of data and this type of work?**

If the answer is no or unclear, do not use it for company data.

**This applies to:**

- AI tools
    
- Cloud storage platforms
    
- File-transfer services
    
- Browser extensions
    
- Productivity apps
    
- Note-taking tools
    
- Design tools
    
- Translation tools
    
- PDF converters
    
- Screen recording tools
    
- Meeting transcription tools
    
- Project management apps
    
- Customer support tools
    
- Third-party integrations
    
- Mobile apps
    
- Personal accounts
    

The issue is not whether the tool is popular. The issue is whether the company has reviewed and approved it.

### What Makes a Tool “Approved”?

A tool is approved when the company has reviewed it and decided that it is acceptable for defined business use.

**Approval should consider:**

- What the tool does
    
- What data it can access
    
- What data employees may upload
    
- Where the data is stored
    
- Whether the vendor uses the data for training or improvement
    
- Whether data can be deleted
    
- Whether access can be controlled
    
- Whether MFA is available
    
- Whether logs are available
    
- Whether the tool meets security and privacy requirements
    
- Whether the contract protects company data
    
- Whether the tool is suitable for sensitive information
    
- Who owns the tool internally
    
- How users are added and removed
    

A tool may be approved for one type of data but not another.

**Example:**

A design tool may be approved for public marketing graphics but not for customer data, HR records, legal documents, or confidential strategy.

### Approved Does Not Mean Unlimited

Even approved tools have rules.

**Employees should understand:**

- Some tools are approved only for public or internal information.
    
- Some tools are approved for confidential information.
    
- Some tools are approved only for specific departments.
    
- Some tools may not be approved for personal data.
    
- Some tools may not be approved for regulated data.
    
- Some AI tools may be approved only when sensitive details are removed.
    
- Some tools may be approved for viewing but not uploading.
    
- Some tools may be approved for employees but not contractors.
    

**The correct question is not only:**

“Is this tool approved?”

**The better question is:**

**“Is this tool approved for this specific data and this specific task?”**

### Common Tool Risks

#### 1. Personal Cloud Storage

Examples include personal file drives, personal backup services, or consumer storage accounts.

**Risk:**

Company data may be stored outside company control, shared accidentally, retained after employment ends, or accessed from unmanaged devices.

**Correct behavior:**

Use only company-approved storage locations.

#### 2. Personal Email

Employees may email files to themselves for convenience.

**Risk:**

Personal email may lack company security, retention, monitoring, access control, and incident response coverage.

**Correct behavior:**

Do not send company files to personal email accounts.

#### 3. File Conversion and PDF Tools

Free online tools may ask users to upload documents for conversion, compression, signing, splitting, translation, or formatting.

**Risk:**

The uploaded document may contain confidential or personal information and may be stored by the external service.

**Correct behavior:**

Use approved company tools for document conversion and editing.

#### 4. Browser Extensions

Browser extensions can sometimes read website content, capture data, change pages, track activity, or access browser sessions.

**Risk:**

A malicious or poorly secured extension may expose company data or credentials.

**Correct behavior:**

Install only approved extensions from trusted sources and report suspicious behavior.

#### 5. Note-Taking and Productivity Apps

Employees may copy meeting notes, customer information, strategy documents, or task lists into unapproved apps.

**Risk:**

Sensitive data may be synced to personal accounts or external platforms.

**Correct behavior:**

Use approved company note-taking and productivity tools.

#### 6. Meeting Transcription and Recording Tools

Meeting tools may capture sensitive conversations, screen shares, names, customer data, legal discussions, or strategic decisions.

**Risk:**

Recordings and transcripts may be stored externally, shared broadly, or retained longer than intended.

**Correct behavior:**

Use approved tools and follow company rules for recording, consent, storage, and sharing.

#### 7. Third-Party App Integrations

Some tools ask to connect to email, calendars, cloud files, CRM systems, or chat platforms.

**Risk:**

The app may receive broad access to company data.

**Correct behavior:**

Do not approve app integrations unless they are approved and the requested permissions make sense.

#### 8. AI Tools

AI tools may process prompts, uploaded files, images, code, transcripts, customer records, contracts, or internal strategy.

**Risk:**

Sensitive data may be exposed, retained, reviewed, reused, or mishandled depending on the service and configuration.

**Correct behavior:**

Use only approved AI services and follow company rules about what data may be entered.

### AI Services: Special Rules

AI tools deserve special attention because they make it very easy to share large amounts of company information quickly.

**Employees may use AI for:**

- Drafting emails
    
- Summarizing documents
    
- Rewriting text
    
- Translating content
    
- Analyzing spreadsheets
    
- Creating presentations
    
- Writing code
    
- Reviewing contracts
    
- Summarizing meetings
    
- Generating customer responses
    
- Brainstorming ideas
    
- Creating policies or reports
    

These uses can be valuable, but they can also create risk.

**Before using AI with company information, employees should ask:**

1. Is this AI tool approved by the company?
    
2. Am I using a company-approved account?
    
3. Is the information public, internal, confidential, or restricted?
    
4. Does the prompt contain customer, employee, financial, legal, security, or confidential data?
    
5. Can I remove names, account numbers, addresses, contract terms, pricing, credentials, or internal notes?
    
6. Is the output going to be checked by a human before use?
    
7. Could the output be wrong, biased, misleading, or incomplete?
    
8. Does the tool have permission to access files, email, chats, or calendars?
    
9. Could the information create harm if it appeared outside the company?
    
10. Does company policy allow this use?
    

If unsure, do not paste or upload the information.

### What Not to Enter Into Unapproved AI Tools

**Employees should not enter the following into unapproved AI tools:**

- Customer names or records
    
- Employee records
    
- Payroll data
    
- Bank details
    
- Financial forecasts
    
- Contracts
    
- Legal advice
    
- Board materials
    
- Confidential strategy
    
- Source code
    
- Passwords
    
- MFA codes
    
- API keys
    
- Access tokens
    
- Security findings
    
- Vulnerability reports
    
- Incident details
    
- Supplier pricing
    
- Unreleased announcements
    
- Internal investigations
    
- Regulated data
    
- Personal information
    
- Proprietary processes
    

A prompt can be a data disclosure.

An uploaded file can be a data transfer.

An AI integration can be a third-party access grant.

### AI Output Must Be Checked

AI risk is not only about input. It is also about output.

**AI-generated output can be:**

- Incorrect
    
- Outdated
    
- Misleading
    
- Overconfident
    
- Incomplete
    
- Biased
    
- Unsourced
    
- Inconsistent with company policy
    
- Inconsistent with legal or regulatory requirements
    
- Inappropriate for customers
    
- Based on misunderstood instructions
    

Employees should not treat AI output as automatically correct.

**Before using AI output, check:**

- Is the information accurate?
    
- Are facts verified?
    
- Is confidential information included accidentally?
    
- Is the tone appropriate?
    
- Does it match company policy?
    
- Does it require legal, HR, compliance, technical, or management review?
    
- Is it safe to send externally?
    
- Is it clear that a human remains responsible?
    

AI can assist work. It should not replace judgment.

### AI and Customer Communication

Be careful when using AI to draft messages to customers, suppliers, employees, or regulators.

Employees should not send AI-generated communication without review.

**Check for:**

- Incorrect promises
    
- Unsupported claims
    
- Wrong pricing
    
- Confidential details
    
- Unapproved legal language
    
- Inaccurate technical statements
    
- Sensitive customer information
    
- Tone problems
    
- Missing context
    
- Hallucinated facts
    

The employee remains responsible for what is sent.

### AI and Code

If employees or technical staff use AI to generate code, scripts, formulas, or automation, extra review is needed.

**AI-generated code may contain:**

- Security weaknesses
    
- Hidden logic errors
    
- Licensing issues
    
- Poor handling of sensitive data
    
- Unsafe dependencies
    
- Insecure configuration
    
- Hardcoded secrets
    
- Unreliable assumptions
    

Code or automation generated with AI should be reviewed before use, especially if it touches company systems, customer data, security controls, financial processes, or production environments.

### AI and Confidential Documents

AI summarization can be useful, but document uploads are risky.

**Before uploading a document to AI, ask:**

- Is the AI service approved for this document type?
    
- Does the document contain personal data?
    
- Does the document contain confidential terms?
    
- Does the document contain legal, HR, financial, or security details?
    
- Can the sensitive parts be removed first?
    
- Does the company allow this document to be processed externally?
    
- Who will review the output?
    

If the document is confidential or restricted, do not upload it unless the tool and use case are approved.

### Tool Approval Decision Rule

**Before using a tool with company data, ask:**

1. Is this tool approved?
    
2. Is this account company-controlled?
    
3. Is the data type allowed in this tool?
    
4. Is the data sensitive?
    
5. Does the tool request access to other company systems?
    
6. Does the tool store, sync, or retain the data?
    
7. Can the company remove access later?
    
8. Would IT or leadership know where this data went?
    
9. Would we be comfortable explaining this use to a customer, auditor, regulator, or executive?
    
10. Is there a safer approved alternative?
    

If the answer creates doubt, stop and ask.

### What Employees Should Do

Employees should:

1. Use only approved tools for company information.
    
2. Use company-controlled accounts, not personal accounts, for work.
    
3. Check whether a tool is approved for the data being handled.
    
4. Use approved AI tools only within company rules.
    
5. Remove sensitive details before using AI where appropriate.
    
6. Avoid uploading confidential files to unapproved tools.
    
7. Avoid granting third-party apps broad permissions.
    
8. Report suspicious app-permission requests.
    
9. Ask before installing browser extensions.
    
10. Use approved file-sharing, storage, and collaboration tools.
    
11. Verify AI-generated output before using it.
    
12. Report accidental use of unapproved tools with sensitive data.
    

### What Employees Should Not Do

**Employees should not:**

- Upload company files to personal cloud storage
    
- Send work files to personal email accounts
    
- Use public AI tools with confidential or restricted information
    
- Paste customer, employee, legal, financial, or security data into unapproved AI tools
    
- Install unapproved browser extensions
    
- Approve third-party app access without review
    
- Use free online converters for sensitive documents
    
- Record or transcribe sensitive meetings with unapproved tools
    
- Store passwords, notes, or customer records in personal apps
    
- Connect personal productivity tools to work email or files
    
- Use AI output externally without review
    
- Assume a popular tool is safe because many people use it
    

### If You Already Used an Unapproved Tool

Employees should report quickly if they accidentally used an unapproved tool with company information.

**They should provide:**

- Which tool was used
    
- What information was entered or uploaded
    
- Whether files were attached
    
- Whether personal, customer, employee, legal, financial, or security data was involved
    
- Whether the tool was connected to company accounts
    
- When the use occurred
    
- Whether the data can be deleted from the tool
    
- Whether anyone else received the output
    

Do not hide the mistake. The company may be able to reduce the impact if it knows quickly.

### Manager Responsibilities

Managers should make sure productivity pressure does not push staff into unsafe tools.

Managers should:

- Tell teams which tools are approved
    
- Make sure staff know where to check approved tools
    
- Avoid asking employees to use personal accounts for company work
    
- Avoid encouraging unapproved AI use for speed
    
- Review team workflows for shadow IT
    
- Ensure vendors and contractors use approved collaboration methods
    
- Escalate tool needs instead of allowing unsafe workarounds
    
- Make sure sensitive data is not copied into unmanaged systems
    
- Reinforce AI rules with practical examples
    
- Support employees who pause before using a questionable tool
    
- Report recurring tool gaps to leadership
    

Managers should not treat unapproved tool use as harmless innovation. It may be a data-governance failure.

### Executive and Key Staff Assurance Points

Executives and key staff should verify that the company governs tools and AI services intentionally.

Leadership should ensure the company has:

- An approved application list
    
- A process for requesting new tools
    
- A process for reviewing vendor security and privacy
    
- A software and cloud service inventory
    
- Rules for personal account use
    
- Rules for browser extensions
    
- Rules for file-transfer and conversion tools
    
- Rules for AI tool use
    
- Defined data types allowed in each tool
    
- Review of third-party app permissions
    
- Monitoring or review for shadow IT where practical
    
- Contractual protections for vendors handling sensitive data
    
- Offboarding process for tool access
    
- A process for deleting or exporting company data from tools
    
- Training on AI input and output risks
    

The leadership question is:

**Do we know which tools process company information, and are those tools approved for that data?**


### Evidence Leaders Should Request

Useful evidence includes:

- Approved application list
    
- Software and cloud service inventory
    
- AI use policy
    
- Approved AI tool list
    
- Data classification rules for AI use
    
- Vendor security review records
    
- Third-party app permission reports
    
- Browser extension inventory
    
- File-sharing and file-transfer policy
    
- Meeting recording and transcription policy
    
- Personal account use policy
    
- Tool request and approval workflow
    
- Software procurement process
    
- Records of shadow IT findings
    
- Incident reports involving unapproved tools
    
- Training completion records
    
- Offboarding checklist covering SaaS tools
    

Leadership should not accept “employees know what tools to use” as enough. The company needs a visible, current, and enforced tool governance process.

### Warning Signs of Weak Tool and AI Governance

The company may have a problem if:

- Employees do not know which tools are approved
    
- Staff use personal email or cloud storage for work
    
- Teams buy software without review
    
- AI tools are used without clear rules
    
- Employees paste sensitive data into public AI tools
    
- Browser extensions are installed freely
    
- Third-party apps can connect to email or files without approval
    
- Contractors use their own tools for company data
    
- Meeting transcripts are stored in unknown systems
    
- Free online converters are used for confidential documents
    
- No one owns the application inventory
    
- Former employees retain access to SaaS tools
    
- Vendors receive data before security review
    
- Managers encourage “whatever tool gets it done fastest”
    

These are serious risks because company data may be leaving approved environments without visibility.

### Examples


#### Example: Free PDF Converter

An employee needs to compress a contract PDF before sending it to a customer. They upload the contract to a free online PDF converter.

The contract includes pricing, legal terms, customer names, and confidential project details.

**Risk:** - The file has been uploaded to an unapproved external service.

**Safer approach:** - Use a company-approved PDF tool or ask IT for the approved method.

#### Example: Public AI Tool

An employee pastes a customer support ticket into a public AI tool to create a polished response.

The ticket includes the customer’s name, account number, complaint details, internal notes, and refund discussion.

**Risk:** - Sensitive customer and business information may have been disclosed to an unapproved AI service.

**Safer approach:** - Use an approved AI tool if available and remove unnecessary sensitive details. Have a human review the final response.

#### Example: Browser Extension

An employee installs a free browser extension that promises to summarize web pages and emails.

The extension requests permission to read and change data on all websites.

**Risk:** - The extension may be able to access company web apps, email, or sensitive browser content.

**Safer approach:** - Do not install unapproved extensions. Ask IT or security whether the extension is allowed.

#### Example: Personal Cloud Storage

An employee uploads work files to a personal cloud drive so they can work from home.

The files include customer reports and internal planning documents.

**Risk:** - The files are outside company control, may be synced to personal devices, and may remain accessible after the employee leaves.

**Safer approach:** - Use the approved company cloud storage or remote access method.

#### Example: AI Meeting Transcription

A team uses an unapproved AI meeting assistant to record and summarize a client strategy meeting.

The meeting includes pricing, contract terms, product plans, and customer concerns.

**Risk:** - The meeting transcript may be stored by an unapproved service and may expose confidential information.

**Safer approach:** - Use only approved meeting tools and follow recording, consent, storage, and sharing rules.

### Key Takeaway

Approved tools are part of data protection.

Employees should not upload, paste, store, sync, or connect company information to unapproved tools or AI services. Managers should prevent productivity pressure from creating unsafe shortcuts. Executives should verify that the company has a current application inventory, approved AI rules, vendor review, and controls over shadow IT.

**If company data goes into an unapproved tool, the company may lose visibility, control, and protection.**
