### Goal

Staff need to recognize sensitive information before they can protect it, while leadership needs to verify the company has a usable data classification and ownership model.

Make sure to identify customer data, employee data, financial records, contracts, credentials, intellectual property, internal strategy, security information, and regulated records.

Employees cannot protect information they do not recognize.

### Learning Objective

By the end of this section, we will be able to recognize common types of sensitive business information, understand why mishandling that information creates risk, and know when to slow down before sending, uploading, sharing, copying, or entering information into a tool.

**Employees should learn this core behavior:** - Before sharing or using information, ask whether it is sensitive and whether the recipient, system, or tool is approved to receive it.

**Managers and executives should learn this assurance principle:** - The company must know what sensitive information it holds, where it is stored, who owns it, and how it should be protected.

### Why This Matters

Not all company information carries the same level of risk.

Some information can be shared widely with little harm. Other information could damage the company, customers, employees, suppliers, or partners if it is exposed, changed, misused, or sent to the wrong place.

**Sensitive information may appear in:**

- Emails
    
- Attachments
    
- Cloud folders
    
- Shared drives
    
- Chat messages
    
- CRM records
    
- Finance systems
    
- HR systems
    
- Contracts
    
- Spreadsheets
    
- Presentations
    
- Screenshots
    
- Meeting notes
    
- AI prompts
    
- Project management tools
    
- Vendor portals
    
- Printed documents
    

A data leak does not always happen because of a hacker. It can happen because someone sends a spreadsheet to the wrong person, shares a folder too broadly, pastes confidential information into an unapproved AI tool, or uploads customer data into an unapproved application.

The first step is recognition.

If employees cannot identify sensitive information, they cannot protect it.

### Core Rule

Before handling information, ask:

**Would it matter if this information were exposed, changed, lost, copied, or sent to the wrong person?**

If the answer is yes, treat it as sensitive.

### What Is Sensitive Information?

Sensitive information is any information that could create harm, legal exposure, financial loss, privacy risk, reputational damage, security risk, or operational disruption if it is disclosed, altered, lost, or misused.

**Sensitive information may relate to:**

- People
    
- Money
    
- Customers
    
- Employees
    
- Suppliers
    
- Business strategy
    
- Legal matters
    
- Security
    
- Credentials
    
- Intellectual property
    
- Operations
    
- Regulated activities
    

A document does not need to be marked “confidential” to be sensitive. Employees must learn to recognize sensitivity from the content itself.

### Major Categories of Sensitive Information

#### 1. Personal Information

Personal information is information that identifies, relates to, or can be linked to a person.

Examples include:

- Full names
    
- Home addresses
    
- Personal phone numbers
    
- Personal email addresses
    
- Dates of birth
    
- Government identification numbers
    
- Passport or driver’s license details
    
- Employee IDs
    
- Customer IDs
    
- Photographs
    
- Location data
    
- IP addresses where linked to a person
    
- Personal account details
    
- Emergency contact information
    

**Why it is sensitive:**

Personal information can expose individuals to privacy harm, fraud, identity theft, harassment, or unwanted disclosure.

**Correct behavior:**

Handle personal information only through approved systems and only share it with people who have a valid business need.

#### 2. Employee and HR Information

Employee information is especially sensitive because it may involve personal, financial, disciplinary, or legal matters.

**Examples include:**

- Salary and compensation records
    
- Payroll files
    
- Bank details
    
- Tax forms
    
- Performance reviews
    
- Disciplinary records
    
- Medical leave information
    
- Benefits information
    
- Employment contracts
    
- Background check information
    
- Immigration or visa records
    
- Internal complaints
    
- Termination records
    

**Why it is sensitive:**

Exposure could harm employees, create legal risk, damage trust, and expose the company to regulatory or contractual consequences.

**Correct behavior:**

Do not send HR information through informal channels. Verify the recipient and use approved HR or secure file-sharing systems.

#### 3. Customer and Client Information

Customer information may include business records, contact information, account data, transaction details, support records, and confidential project information.

**Examples include:**

- Customer names and contact details
    
- Account numbers
    
- Purchase history
    
- Support tickets
    
- Contracts
    
- Pricing
    
- Customer credentials
    
- Customer files
    
- Implementation details
    
- Confidential business requirements
    
- Customer complaints
    
- Customer financial information
    

**Why it is sensitive:**

Customer information can damage customer trust, violate contracts, expose the customer to risk, and harm the company’s reputation.

**Correct behavior:**

Share customer information only with approved recipients and approved tools. Do not move customer information into personal storage, unapproved apps, or public AI tools.

#### 4. Financial Information

Financial information can be exploited for fraud, theft, manipulation, or competitive harm.

**Examples include:**

- Bank account details
    
- Invoices
    
- Payment instructions
    
- Wire transfer details
    
- Credit card information
    
- Tax records
    
- Revenue reports
    
- Budgets
    
- Forecasts
    
- Profit margins
    
- Pricing models
    
- Supplier payment data
    
- Expense records
    
- Financial statements before release
    

**Why it is sensitive:**

Exposure or alteration of financial information can lead to direct financial loss, fraud, legal risk, or reputational harm.

**Correct behavior:**

Treat financial documents and payment instructions as high-risk. Verify unusual requests through a separate trusted channel.

#### 5. Legal, Contractual, and Compliance Information

Legal information may include obligations, disputes, negotiations, claims, or confidential communications.

**Examples include:**

- Contracts
    
- Legal opinions
    
- Settlement discussions
    
- Regulatory filings
    
- Compliance evidence
    
- Audit reports
    
- Investigation records
    
- Non-disclosure agreements
    
- Insurance claims
    
- Litigation documents
    
- Board materials
    
- Policy exceptions
    
- Risk assessments
    

**Why it is sensitive:**

Legal and compliance information can create regulatory, contractual, reputational, and strategic risk if mishandled.

**Correct behavior:**

Do not forward legal or compliance documents casually. Use approved channels and confirm authorization before sharing.

#### 6. Intellectual Property and Business Strategy

Intellectual property and strategy information can give competitors or attackers insight into the company’s plans, products, customers, and operations.

**Examples include:**

- Product roadmaps
    
- Source code
    
- Technical designs
    
- Research and development documents
    
- Proprietary processes
    
- Marketing plans
    
- Sales strategy
    
- Customer lists
    
- Pricing strategy
    
- Acquisition or partnership plans
    
- Internal forecasts
    
- Unreleased announcements
    
- Trade secrets
    

**Why it is sensitive:**

Exposure may reduce competitive advantage, damage negotiations, or reveal company plans.

**Correct behavior:**

Treat internal strategy and proprietary work as sensitive even if it does not contain personal information.

#### 7. Security and IT Information

Security information can help attackers understand how the company is protected and where it may be vulnerable.

**Examples include:**

- Passwords
    
- MFA recovery codes
    
- API keys
    
- Access tokens
    
- VPN details
    
- Network diagrams
    
- Firewall rules
    
- Vulnerability scan results
    
- Penetration test reports
    
- Incident reports
    
- Backup locations
    
- Security configurations
    
- Administrator account lists
    
- Internal IP addresses
    
- System architecture diagrams
    

**Why it is sensitive:**

Security information can make attacks easier, faster, and more damaging.

**Correct behavior:**

Never share security information through informal channels. Do not paste credentials, keys, or security details into chat tools, documents, tickets, or AI systems unless specifically approved.

#### 8. Operational and Supplier Information

Operational information can expose how the company works, what it depends on, and where disruption would hurt.

**Examples include:**

- Supplier lists
    
- Vendor contracts
    
- Delivery schedules
    
- System dependencies
    
- Business continuity plans
    
- Production schedules
    
- Inventory levels
    
- Logistics information
    
- Critical process documentation
    
- Procurement records
    
- Facility access information
    
- Internal escalation lists
    

**Why it is sensitive:**

Attackers can use operational information for fraud, disruption, extortion, or targeted social engineering.

**Correct behavior:**

Be cautious when sharing supplier, logistics, procurement, or operational details externally.

#### 9. Authentication and Access Information

Authentication and access information deserves special treatment because it can allow direct entry into company systems.

**Examples include:**

- Passwords
    
- MFA codes
    
- Recovery codes
    
- Security keys
    
- Passkeys
    
- API keys
    
- Session tokens
    
- Login links
    
- Admin credentials
    
- Shared account details
    
- Password reset links
    
- Temporary access links
    

**Why it is sensitive:**

This information can be used to bypass normal access controls.

**Correct behavior:**

Never share authentication information. Report any request for passwords, MFA codes, recovery codes, or access tokens.

#### 10. AI Prompts and Uploaded AI Content

Information entered into AI tools can be sensitive, even if it is not in a traditional document.

**Examples include:**

- Customer records pasted into a prompt
    
- Internal strategy summarized for an AI tool
    
- Employee information used for drafting
    
- Contract text uploaded for review
    
- Source code entered into an AI assistant
    
- Meeting notes containing confidential details
    
- Security findings pasted for analysis
    
- Financial reports uploaded for summarization
    

**Why it is sensitive:**

AI tools may process, store, review, or retain information depending on the service and configuration. Even when a tool is legitimate, employees must use only approved tools and approved data types.

**Correct behavior:**

Do not paste, upload, or summarize sensitive company information in unapproved AI tools.

### Sensitivity Is About Context

Some information may seem harmless by itself but become sensitive when combined with other information.

**Example:**

A first name alone may not be highly sensitive. A first name combined with salary, address, bank details, health information, or disciplinary records becomes sensitive.

**Another example:**

A supplier name may not be sensitive by itself. A supplier name combined with payment instructions, delivery schedules, pricing, and executive approval details may become sensitive.

**The rule is:**

**Do not judge sensitivity from one field alone. Consider the full context.**

### Common Places Sensitive Information Hides

Sensitive information is often stored in places employees do not think about.

**Examples include:**

- Spreadsheet tabs
    
- Hidden columns
    
- File names
    
- Comments in documents
    
- Email threads
    
- Message history
    
- Screenshots
    
- Calendar invites
    
- Meeting transcripts
    
- Chat attachments
    
- Download folders
    
- Printer trays
    
- Shared-drive archives
    
- Old project folders
    
- Backup exports
    
- CRM notes
    
- Support tickets
    
- AI prompt history
    

Employees should not assume that only the visible main document matters.

### Simple Classification Model

For a SME, keep classification simple. Overly complex classification systems usually fail.

A practical model could use four levels:

#### Public

Information approved for public release.

Examples:

- Published website content
    
- Public marketing materials
    
- Press releases
    
- Public job postings
    

**Handling rule:**

Can be shared externally, but should still be accurate and approved.

#### Internal

Information intended for employees or approved internal users.

**Examples:**

- Internal announcements
    
- General policies
    
- Team updates
    
- Internal process notes
    

**Handling rule:**

Do not share externally unless approved.

#### Confidential

Information that could harm the company, customers, employees, or partners if exposed.

**Examples:**

- Customer lists
    
- Contracts
    
- Financial reports
    
- HR files
    
- Supplier pricing
    
- Internal strategy
    
- Project documents
    

**Handling rule:**

Share only with approved people who need it. Use approved systems and secure sharing methods.

#### Restricted

Highly sensitive information requiring the strongest controls.

**Examples:**

- Passwords
    
- MFA recovery codes
    
- Bank details
    
- Payroll files
    
- Legal investigations
    
- Security reports
    
- Admin credentials
    
- Regulated records
    
- Incident response details
    
- API keys
    

**Handling rule:**

Access should be limited, logged where possible, and shared only through specifically approved processes.

### The Employee Sensitivity Check

Before sharing, uploading, copying, printing, or entering information into a tool, employees should ask:

1. Does this contain personal information?
    
2. Does this contain customer or employee data?
    
3. Does this contain financial, legal, or HR information?
    
4. Does this contain passwords, keys, access codes, or security details?
    
5. Does this reveal internal strategy, pricing, suppliers, or operations?
    
6. Would the company care if this became public?
    
7. Would a customer, employee, regulator, supplier, or attacker care if this was exposed?
    
8. Am I using an approved system or tool?
    
9. Does the recipient actually need this information?
    
10. Is there a safer way to share less information?
    

If the answer to any question suggests risk, treat the information as sensitive and slow down and consider the proper channel and method to be used.

### What Employees Should Do

**Employees should:**

1. Learn the company’s data classification labels.
    
2. Treat personal, customer, HR, financial, legal, security, and access information as sensitive.
    
3. Use approved systems for storing and sharing sensitive information.
    
4. Verify recipients before sending sensitive data.
    
5. Share only what is necessary.
    
6. Use view-only access where appropriate.
    
7. Avoid copying sensitive information into new locations unnecessarily.
    
8. Avoid downloading sensitive data unless needed.
    
9. Avoid printing sensitive information unless necessary.
    
10. Report accidental exposure immediately.
    
11. Ask before using unapproved tools for sensitive data.
    
12. Never paste credentials, keys, or confidential data into unapproved AI tools.
    

### What Employees Should Not Do

Employees should not:

- Assume information is safe because it is not marked confidential
    
- Send sensitive files to personal email
    
- Store customer or HR data on personal devices
    
- Upload company files to unapproved cloud tools
    
- Paste sensitive information into public AI tools
    
- Share folders broadly without checking contents
    
- Send spreadsheets without checking hidden tabs or columns
    
- Forward long email threads without reviewing included information
    
- Leave printed sensitive documents unattended
    
- Use public links for confidential files unless specifically approved
    
- Share passwords, MFA codes, recovery codes, or API keys
    
- Ignore accidental data exposure
    

### Manager Responsibilities

Managers should ensure their teams can recognize the information they handle.

**Managers should:**

- Identify which sensitive information their team uses
    
- Explain what information is public, internal, confidential, or restricted
    
- Make sure new employees understand data-handling expectations
    
- Confirm that team files are stored in approved locations
    
- Review whether sensitive files are shared too broadly
    
- Ensure contractors understand data restrictions
    
- Encourage employees to ask before sharing uncertain information
    
- Make sure sensitive data is removed from old project spaces when no longer needed
    
- Report data-handling gaps to leadership, IT, legal, or compliance
    
- Avoid asking staff to send sensitive data through informal channels
    

Managers should not assume that employees know what is sensitive. They must make it explicit.

### Executive and Key Staff Assurance Points

Executives and key staff should verify that sensitive information is not managed informally.

Leadership should ensure the company has:

- A simple data classification model
    
- Clear examples of sensitive information
    
- Named owners for important data sets
    
- Approved storage locations for sensitive information
    
- Approved sharing methods
    
- Rules for external sharing
    
- Rules for AI tool use
    
- Rules for personal data handling
    
- Rules for financial, HR, legal, and security information
    
- A process for reporting data exposure
    
- Periodic review of sensitive data locations
    
- Vendor controls for sensitive data shared with third parties
    
- Retention and deletion rules for data no longer needed
    

The executive question is:

**Do we know what sensitive information we hold, where it is, who can access it, and how it is protected?**

### Questions Leaders Should Ask

Executives and key staff should ask:

1. What types of sensitive information does the company hold?
    
2. Where is sensitive information stored?
    
3. Who owns each major category of sensitive information?
    
4. Who can access customer, employee, financial, legal, and security data?
    
5. Is there a simple classification system employees can understand?
    
6. Are employees trained with examples from their actual work?
    
7. Are sensitive files stored in approved systems?
    
8. Are personal email, personal cloud storage, and unapproved apps restricted?
    
9. Are AI tools approved and governed?
    
10. Do employees know what not to paste into AI tools?
    
11. Are external sharing permissions reviewed?
    
12. Are sensitive data exposures reported and investigated?
    
13. Are old files and project folders reviewed or deleted when no longer needed?
    
14. Are vendors required to protect sensitive data contractually and technically?
    
15. Can the company produce an inventory of critical data locations?
    

### Evidence Leaders Should Request

Useful evidence includes:

- Data classification policy
    
- Sensitive data inventory
    
- Data owner list
    
- Approved storage locations
    
- Approved application list
    
- Cloud sharing reports
    
- External user access reports
    
- AI use policy
    
- Vendor data-processing or data-handling records
    
- Data retention policy
    
- Records of data exposure incidents
    
- Training completion records
    
- Examples of role-specific data-handling guidance
    
- Reports showing where confidential or restricted files are stored
    
- Evidence that old or unnecessary sensitive data is deleted or archived securely
    

Leadership should not accept “employees know to be careful” as enough. Sensitive information must be identified, owned, and governed.

### Warning Signs of Weak Data Awareness

The company may have weak data awareness if:

- Employees do not know what counts as sensitive
    
- Sensitive files are spread across many tools
    
- Staff use personal email for work files
    
- HR, finance, customer, and legal files are stored casually
    
- Shared folders contain old confidential data
    
- External users remain in project folders after work ends
    
- AI tools are used without clear rules
    
- People rely on file names instead of content review
    
- Employees send spreadsheets without checking hidden tabs
    
- There is no clear process for reporting accidental exposure
    
- Managers cannot say where their team’s sensitive data is stored
    
- Leadership has no inventory of critical data
    

These are not just administrative weaknesses. They are data breach conditions.

#### Example: The Hidden Spreadsheet Tab

An employee sends a spreadsheet to a supplier. The visible tab contains only project quantities. A hidden tab contains customer pricing, margin calculations, and internal notes.

**Risk:** - The employee intended to share only basic project information but accidentally exposed confidential financial and customer data.

**Correct behavior:** - Review spreadsheets before sharing. Check tabs, hidden columns, comments, and embedded data. Share only the information the recipient needs.

#### Example: The AI Prompt

An employee copies a customer complaint file into an unapproved AI tool and asks it to summarize the issue.

The file includes the customer’s name, email address, account number, contract details, and internal escalation notes.

**Risk:** - Sensitive customer and business information has been entered into an unapproved external tool.

**Correct behavior:** - Use only approved AI tools. Remove sensitive information where possible. Follow company policy before entering customer or confidential data.

#### Example: The Shared Folder

A manager creates a folder called “Board Materials” and shares it with the whole department to make collaboration easier.

The folder includes financial forecasts, strategy documents, acquisition notes, and legal correspondence.

**Risk:** - Too many employees have access to highly sensitive information.

**Correct behavior:** - Restrict access to those with a clear need. Use appropriate permissions and review access regularly.

#### Example: The Long Email Thread

An employee forwards a long email thread to an external consultant. The latest message is harmless, but earlier messages include pricing, employee names, and legal advice.

**Risk:** - Sensitive information was disclosed because the employee did not review the full thread.

**Correct behavior:** - Review the entire message history before forwarding. Remove unnecessary sensitive information or start a new message with only the required content.

### Key Takeaway

Sensitive information is not always obvious. It may appear in files, emails, screenshots, spreadsheets, cloud folders, chat messages, AI prompts, and old project spaces.

Employees should learn to recognize personal, customer, employee, financial, legal, security, operational, and confidential business information before sharing or using it. Managers should make sensitivity clear for their teams. Executives should verify that the company has a practical classification system, data owners, approved storage locations, and clear rules for sharing and AI use.

**You cannot protect sensitive information if you do not first recognize it.**
