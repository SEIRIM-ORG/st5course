### Learning Objective

By the end of this section, we will understand that safe data handling is not only an individual responsibility. It also requires company-level governance: clear ownership, classification, approved storage, sharing rules, retention practices, vendor controls, AI rules, and incident response procedures.

Employees should learn this core behavior:

**Follow the company’s data rules, use approved systems, and report unclear or risky data-handling situations.**

Managers and executives should learn this assurance principle:

**The company must know what sensitive data it has, where it is stored, who owns it, who can access it, how it is shared, how long it is kept, and how data risks are reduced.**

### Why This Matters

Data is one of the company’s most valuable assets and one of its largest sources of risk.

A company may hold customer records, employee information, financial data, legal documents, supplier details, contracts, intellectual property, system information, security reports, and confidential business strategy.

**If that information is mishandled, the company may suffer:**

- Financial loss
    
- Fraud
    
- Data breach costs
    
- Legal or regulatory exposure
    
- Loss of customer trust
    
- Operational disruption
    
- Contractual consequences
    
- Reputational damage
    
- Competitive harm
    
- Increased cyberattack risk
    

Individual employees can reduce risk through careful handling, but that is not enough. The company needs clear data governance.

Without governance, data spreads everywhere: old folders, personal drives, shared links, chat tools, email threads, SaaS platforms, AI tools, vendor systems, laptops, and forgotten exports.

The danger is simple:

**If the company does not govern data, data governs the company’s risk.**

### Core Rule

Data governance means making sure important information is identified, owned, classified, protected, shared appropriately, retained only as needed, and disposed of safely.

The practical rule is:

**Sensitive data must have an owner, an approved location, defined access, clear handling rules, and a lifecycle.**

### What Is Data Governance?

Data governance is the set of decisions, responsibilities, policies, processes, and controls that determine how company data is handled.

**It answers questions such as:**

- What sensitive data do we have?
    
- Where is it stored?
    
- Who owns it?
    
- Who can access it?
    
- Who approves access?
    
- How should it be classified?
    
- How should it be shared?
    
- Which tools may process it?
    
- Which vendors may receive it?
    
- How long should it be kept?
    
- When should it be deleted?
    
- What happens if it is exposed?
    
- How do we verify that controls are working?
    

Data governance is not only a compliance exercise. It is a practical business discipline.

### The Data Governance Lifecycle

A company should manage data across its full lifecycle.

#### 1. Create or Collect

The company should collect only data it needs for a defined purpose.

**Questions to ask:**

- Why are we collecting this data?
    
- Is it necessary?
    
- Is the data sensitive?
    
- Is the collection approved?
    
- Are we collecting more than we need?
    
- Do people understand how the data will be used?
    

#### 2. Store

Data should be stored in approved locations.

Questions to ask:

- Is this stored in an approved system?
    
- Is the data encrypted where appropriate?
    
- Who can access it?
    
- Is it backed up?
    
- Is it monitored?
    
- Is it stored in a personal or unmanaged location?
    

#### 3. Use

Data should be used only for approved business purposes.

Questions to ask:

- Is the use consistent with the original purpose?
    
- Does the user need the data?
    
- Is the tool approved?
    
- Is AI being used appropriately?
    
- Is the data being copied unnecessarily?
    

#### 4. Share

Data should be shared only with approved recipients using approved methods.

Questions to ask:

- Who is receiving it?
    
- Why do they need it?
    
- What is the minimum necessary data?
    
- Is the recipient internal or external?
    
- Is the sharing method secure?
    
- Should access expire?
    

#### 5. Retain

Data should be kept only as long as needed for business, legal, regulatory, or contractual reasons.

Questions to ask:

- How long do we need this?
    
- Who owns retention?
    
- Is the retention period documented?
    
- Is old data creating unnecessary risk?
    
- Are duplicate copies being kept without reason?
    

#### 6. Dispose

Data should be deleted, archived, returned, or destroyed safely when no longer needed.

Questions to ask:

- Can this data be deleted?
    
- Does it need to be archived?
    
- Has it been removed from shared locations?
    
- Has vendor-held data been deleted or returned?
    
- Are backups and records handled according to policy?
    

The lifecycle matters because data risk does not end after the first use.

### Key Elements of Data Governance

#### 1. Data Ownership

Important data should have an owner.

**A data owner is responsible for deciding:**

- Who may access the data
    
- How the data should be classified
    
- Where it should be stored
    
- How it may be shared
    
- How long it should be kept
    
- What controls are required
    
- What risks should be escalated
    

**Examples:**

- HR owns employee records.
    
- Finance owns payment and accounting records.
    
- Sales owns CRM data.
    
- Legal owns legal documents.
    
- IT or security owns security logs and technical records.
    
- Operations owns operational and supplier process data.
    

If no one owns a data set, no one is accountable for protecting it.

#### 2. Data Classification

Data classification helps employees understand how information should be handled.

A simple model works best for SMEs.

**Recommended model:**

- **Public:** Approved for public release.
    
- **Internal:** For company use only.
    
- **Confidential:** Could harm the company, customers, employees, or partners if exposed.
    
- **Restricted:** Highly sensitive; requires the strongest controls.
    

The classification system should include practical examples, not just definitions.

Employees should not need to guess whether payroll files, customer exports, security reports, or contracts are sensitive.

#### 3. Approved Storage Locations

Sensitive data should be stored only in approved locations.

**Approved locations may include:**

- Company cloud storage
    
- Approved business applications
    
- Approved HR systems
    
- Approved finance systems
    
- Approved CRM systems
    
- Approved document management systems
    
- Approved secure portals
    
- Approved backup systems
    

**Unapproved locations may include:**

- Personal email
    
- Personal cloud storage
    
- Personal devices
    
- Personal messaging apps
    
- Free file-transfer tools
    
- Unapproved AI tools
    
- Unmanaged USB drives
    
- Informal spreadsheets
    
- Forgotten local folders
    

The rule is simple:

**If sensitive data is outside approved systems, the company may lose control over it.**

#### 4. Access Control

Data governance requires knowing who can access sensitive information.

**Access should be:**

- Approved
    
- Role-based where practical
    
- Limited to business need
    
- Reviewed periodically
    
- Removed when no longer needed
    
- Strongly controlled for restricted data
    
- Logged or monitored where appropriate
    

Access should not be granted because it is convenient or because someone might need it someday.

#### 5. Sharing Rules

The company should define how information can be shared internally and externally.

Sharing rules should cover:

- Email attachments
    
- Cloud links
    
- Shared folders
    
- External users
    
- Vendor portals
    
- Personal email restrictions
    
- Secure file transfer
    
- Public links
    
- AI tools
    
- Chat tools
    
- Screenshots
    
- Printed documents
    
- Meeting recordings and transcripts
    

Employees need practical rules, not vague instructions to “be careful.”

#### 6. Retention and Deletion

Keeping data forever increases risk.

Old data can still be exposed, stolen, misused, or produced during legal or regulatory processes.

**The company should define:**

- What data must be retained
    
- Why it must be retained
    
- How long it must be retained
    
- Who owns retention
    
- When it should be deleted
    
- How deletion should be performed
    
- What happens to data held by vendors
    
- What happens to archived and backup data
    

The safest data is often data the company no longer needs and has properly removed.

#### 7. Vendor and Third-Party Data Risk

Vendors often process company data.

**Examples include:**

- Payroll providers
    
- HR platforms
    
- Cloud services
    
- IT providers
    
- Marketing agencies
    
- CRM platforms
    
- Legal providers
    
- Accounting firms
    
- AI tools
    
- Analytics platforms
    
- Customer support systems
    
- Managed service providers
    

**The company should know:**

- Which vendors receive sensitive data
    
- What data they receive
    
- Why they need it
    
- Where they store it
    
- How they protect it
    
- Whether they subcontract processing
    
- How long they retain it
    
- How data is deleted or returned
    
- What happens if the vendor has a breach
    

Vendor data handling should be reviewed before sensitive information is shared.

#### 8. AI Governance

AI tools need explicit governance because employees may paste, upload, summarize, translate, analyze, or generate content using company information.

**AI governance should define:**

- Which AI tools are approved
    
- Which AI uses are allowed
    
- Which data types may be entered
    
- Which data types are prohibited
    
- Whether prompts and outputs are retained
    
- Whether the tool can train on company data
    
- Whether human review is required
    
- Whether AI output can be sent externally
    
- How AI mistakes or exposures should be reported
    
- Who approves new AI use cases
    

AI governance should not block all AI use. It should make AI use controlled, transparent, and safe.

#### 9. Monitoring and Reporting

The company should have ways to detect and respond to data risk.

**Examples:**

- Reports of public links
    
- External sharing reports
    
- Data loss prevention alerts
    
- Access review reports
    
- Vendor access reviews
    
- Logs of bulk downloads
    
- Reports of unusual file access
    
- Reports of sensitive data in unapproved locations
    
- Incident reports for accidental disclosure
    
- AI use monitoring where appropriate and lawful
    

Employees should also know how to report accidental data exposure quickly.

### What Employees Should Do

**Employees should:**

1. Learn the company’s data classifications.
    
2. Store sensitive information only in approved systems.
    
3. Use approved tools and AI services.
    
4. Share sensitive information only with approved recipients.
    
5. Use the minimum necessary data.
    
6. Avoid personal email, personal cloud storage, and unapproved apps.
    
7. Check permissions before sharing links or folders.
    
8. Delete temporary exports when no longer needed.
    
9. Report accidental sharing immediately.
    
10. Ask when unsure whether data can be shared, uploaded, copied, or retained.
    
11. Follow retention and deletion instructions.
    
12. Respect data owners’ decisions about access and sharing.
    

### What Employees Should Not Do

**Employees should not:**

- Store sensitive files in personal accounts
    
- Upload company data to unapproved tools
    
- Paste confidential information into public AI services
    
- Share folders broadly without approval
    
- Keep old exports indefinitely
    
- Create unofficial data repositories
    
- Download sensitive records without a business need
    
- Send restricted data through informal channels
    
- Use real customer or employee data in demos unless approved
    
- Ignore accidental data exposure
    
- Assume all internal data can be freely shared
    
- Bypass data owners or approved processes
    

### Manager Responsibilities

Managers are responsible for making data governance practical inside their teams.

**Managers should:**

- Identify the sensitive data their team handles
    
- Know where that data is stored
    
- Confirm who owns important team data
    
- Make sure employees understand classification rules
    
- Ensure team data is stored in approved locations
    
- Limit access to current business need
    
- Review external sharing
    
- Remove old or unnecessary data from team spaces
    
- Make sure contractors and vendors receive only approved data
    
- Enforce AI usage rules
    
- Encourage reporting of accidental exposure
    
- Coordinate with IT, security, legal, HR, or compliance when needed
    

Managers should not allow each employee to invent their own data-handling system.

### Executive and Key Staff Assurance Points

Executives and key staff should verify that data governance is not just a written policy.

**Leadership should ensure the company has:**

- A data classification model
    
- Named data owners
    
- An inventory of critical data
    
- Approved storage locations
    
- Access review procedures
    
- External sharing controls
    
- Vendor data-handling requirements
    
- AI governance rules
    
- Retention and deletion schedules
    
- Incident reporting for data exposure
    
- Backup and recovery alignment for critical data
    
- Legal and regulatory input where needed
    
- Periodic management review of data risks
    

The leadership question is:

**Can we prove that sensitive data is identified, owned, controlled, and protected throughout its lifecycle?**

### Evidence Leaders Should Request

Useful evidence includes:

- Data classification policy
    
- Sensitive data inventory
    
- Data owner register
    
- Approved storage location list
    
- Approved application list
    
- Approved AI tool list
    
- AI use policy
    
- Access review reports
    
- External sharing reports
    
- Public link reports
    
- Vendor data-sharing records
    
- Vendor risk assessments
    
- Data retention schedule
    
- Deletion or archival records
    
- Data incident reports
    
- Training completion records
    
- Data loss prevention reports, if available
    
- Cloud storage permission reports
    
- List of known data governance exceptions
    
- Management review notes on data risk
    

Leadership should not accept “we have a policy” as enough. The stronger standard is:

**Show the evidence that the policy is working.**

### Minimum Data Governance Controls for SMEs

An SME does not need a complex enterprise data governance program to improve risk quickly.

**At minimum, the company should have:**

1. A simple data classification model
    
2. A list of sensitive data types
    
3. Named owners for major data sets
    
4. Approved storage locations
    
5. Rules for external sharing
    
6. Rules for AI tool use
    
7. Access review for sensitive systems and folders
    
8. Vendor data-sharing review
    
9. Retention and deletion rules
    
10. A reporting process for accidental exposure
    

These ten controls are enough to move from informal data handling to structured data risk management.

### Warning Signs of Weak Data Governance

The company may have weak data governance if:

- No one owns important data sets
    
- Employees do not know what counts as sensitive
    
- Sensitive files are spread across many tools
    
- Personal email is used for work files
    
- Personal cloud storage contains company information
    
- AI tools are used without rules
    
- Vendors receive data without review
    
- Public links are common
    
- External users are not reviewed
    
- Old exports are kept indefinitely
    
- Contractors keep access after projects end
    
- Data classifications exist but nobody uses them
    
- Retention rules are unclear
    
- Data incidents are hidden or underreported
    
- Leadership cannot say where critical data is stored
    

These are not paperwork problems. They are breach, fraud, and compliance risks.

### Examples


#### Example: No Data Owner

A company has a customer export stored in a shared folder. Sales uses it, marketing copies it, finance adds payment notes, and operations adds delivery details.

Nobody owns the file.

**Risk:** - The file becomes more sensitive over time, access expands, old copies multiply, and no one is accountable for cleanup or protection.

**Better approach:** - Assign a data owner, define the approved storage location, restrict access, remove unnecessary fields, and create rules for export and retention.

#### Example: Uncontrolled AI Use

Employees across the company use different public AI tools to summarize contracts, rewrite customer emails, analyze spreadsheets, and draft HR communications.

There is no approved tool list and no rule about what information may be entered.

**Risk:** - Sensitive information may be disclosed into unapproved systems, and AI-generated output may be used without review.

**Better approach:** - Approve specific AI tools, define allowed use cases, prohibit restricted data, require human review, and train employees on AI data risk.

#### Example: Old External Links

A project folder was shared with external contractors during a six-month project. Two years later, the external users still have access.

The folder now contains additional internal documents unrelated to the original project.

**Risk:** - External access remains open long after the business need ended.

**Better approach:** - Review external access periodically, set expiration dates where possible, and remove users when projects end.

#### Example: Retaining Too Much Data

A team keeps every customer export ever created because “we might need it someday.”

The exports contain customer names, contact details, account IDs, purchase history, and internal notes.

**Risk:** - If the folder is exposed, the breach impact is much larger because old unnecessary data was retained.

**Better approach:** - Define retention periods, delete or archive old exports securely, and keep only what is needed.

### Key Takeaway

Data safety depends on more than individual caution. The company must govern data deliberately.

Employees should follow approved data-handling rules, use approved tools, and report risks. Managers should know what sensitive data their teams handle and where it is stored. Executives should verify that sensitive data has owners, approved locations, access controls, sharing rules, retention periods, vendor controls, AI governance, and incident reporting.

**Good data governance turns sensitive information from an unmanaged liability into a controlled business asset.**
