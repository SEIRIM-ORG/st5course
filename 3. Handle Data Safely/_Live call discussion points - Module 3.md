
### Identify sensitive info

### Discussion Scenario

An employee is preparing a presentation for an external partner. They want to include a project update, a customer example, and a screenshot from an internal dashboard.

The screenshot includes customer names, revenue figures, account status, and internal notes.

Ask learners:

1. What sensitive information appears in the screenshot?
    
2. What could happen if the presentation is shared externally?
    
3. What should the employee remove or mask?
    
4. What approved sharing method should be used?
    
5. Who should approve the final version if customer or financial data remains?
    

Expected answer:

The screenshot contains customer data, financial information, and internal notes. The employee should remove or mask customer names, revenue details, and internal comments unless there is a valid approved reason to share them. The presentation should be reviewed before external sharing and sent through an approved method.

### Use the Minimum Necessary Data



### Discussion Scenario

A sales manager wants to send a customer analysis report to an external agency. The full report includes customer names, email addresses, purchase history, payment status, revenue, and internal sales notes.

The agency only needs to understand purchasing trends by region and product category.

Ask learners:

1. What information does the agency actually need?
    
2. What information should be removed?
    
3. Could aggregated data be used?
    
4. Should customer names and emails be included?
    
5. What approval or vendor process should be followed before sharing?
    
6. How long should the agency retain the data?
    

Expected answer:

The agency likely needs aggregated purchase trends by region and product category. Customer names, emails, payment status, and internal notes should be removed unless there is a specific approved reason to include them. Vendor approval and data-sharing controls should be followed. Retention should be limited to the purpose and agreement.

### Share Data Safely


### Questions Leaders Should Ask

Executives and key staff should ask:

1. What tools are approved for sharing sensitive data?
    
2. Are employees allowed to use personal email or personal cloud storage?
    
3. Can employees create public links?
    
4. Are “anyone with the link” permissions restricted or monitored?
    
5. Are external users reviewed periodically?
    
6. Are shared folders reviewed for excessive access?
    
7. Are sensitive files labeled or classified?
    
8. Are employees trained to check recipients, permissions, and data sensitivity?
    
9. Are vendors required to protect shared data?
    
10. Are AI tools governed?
    
11. Are accidental disclosures reported quickly?
    
12. Are there logs showing who accessed or downloaded sensitive files?
    
13. Are old external links removed?
    
14. Are high-risk departments given role-specific data-sharing guidance?
    




### Evidence Leaders Should Request

Useful evidence includes:

- Data-sharing policy
    
- Approved file-sharing tool list
    
- Cloud-sharing configuration report
    
- External sharing report
    
- Public link report
    
- External user access list
    
- Sensitive data classification policy
    
- AI use policy
    
- Vendor data-sharing requirements
    
- Data loss incident records
    
- Access review records
    
- Secure email or encryption procedure
    
- Training completion records
    
- Examples of role-specific sharing guidance
    
- Evidence that old external access is removed
    

Leadership should not rely on “people know to be careful.” The company needs visible controls around where data goes and who can access it.

### Share Data Safely


### Discussion Scenario

An operations employee needs to share delivery information with an external logistics provider. The easiest option is to send the full customer order export. The file includes customer names, addresses, phone numbers, order values, payment status, internal notes, and delivery locations.

The logistics provider only needs delivery name, delivery location, item count, and scheduled delivery window.

Ask learners:

1. What information does the logistics provider actually need?
    
2. What information should be removed?
    
3. Should the full export be sent?
    
4. What sharing method should be used?
    
5. Should access expire?
    
6. What should be documented or approved?
    

Expected answer:

The provider should receive only the information needed to perform delivery. Payment status, internal notes, order values, and unrelated customer data should be removed. The full export should not be sent. Sharing should happen through an approved method, with access limited to the provider and removed when no longer needed.

### Use Approved Tools and AI Services


### Questions Leaders Should Ask

Executives and key staff should ask:

1. Do we have a current inventory of software, cloud tools, and AI services?
    
2. Which AI tools are approved?
    
3. What types of data may employees enter into approved AI tools?
    
4. Are employees using personal accounts for company work?
    
5. Are public AI tools blocked, governed, or covered by policy?
    
6. Are browser extensions reviewed?
    
7. Are third-party app permissions monitored?
    
8. Are free online file converters restricted for sensitive documents?
    
9. Are meeting transcription tools approved and governed?
    
10. Are vendors reviewed before they receive company data?
    
11. Are contracts in place for tools that process sensitive information?
    
12. Who owns approval of new tools?
    
13. How are employees told which tools are approved?
    
14. How is access removed when employees leave?
    
15. How are unapproved tools detected or reported?



### Discussion Scenario

A manager asks an employee to prepare a summary of a large customer feedback file. The employee finds a free AI tool online that can summarize spreadsheets. The file includes customer names, email addresses, account IDs, complaint details, and internal notes.

Ask learners:

1. What is risky about using the free AI tool?
    
2. What sensitive information is in the file?
    
3. What should the employee check before using any AI tool?
    
4. Could the data be reduced or anonymized?
    
5. What approved alternative should be used?
    
6. What should the manager do differently?
    

Expected answer:

The free AI tool is unapproved and may store or process sensitive customer information outside company control. The file contains personal, customer, and internal business information. The employee should check whether the AI tool is approved, whether that data type is allowed, and whether sensitive fields can be removed. The manager should direct the employee to an approved tool or ask IT, security, privacy, or management for the correct method.

### Establish Data Governance and Mitigate Risk



### Questions Leaders Should Ask

Executives and key staff should ask:

1. What sensitive data do we hold?
    
2. Where is our sensitive data stored?
    
3. Who owns each major data set?
    
4. Who can access customer, employee, financial, legal, and security data?
    
5. Which systems are approved for storing sensitive data?
    
6. Are employees using personal accounts or unapproved tools?
    
7. Are AI tools governed?
    
8. Are vendors reviewed before receiving sensitive data?
    
9. Are external links and shared folders reviewed?
    
10. Are public links restricted or monitored?
    
11. Are old exports and duplicate files cleaned up?
    
12. Do we have retention and deletion rules?
    
13. Are accidental data disclosures reported quickly?
    
14. Can we produce evidence of access reviews?
    
15. Can we produce evidence that data risks are being reduced?

### Establish Data Governance and Mitigate Risk


### Manager and Executive Exercise

Ask managers and executives to answer:

1. What are our top five most sensitive data types?
    
2. Where are they stored?
    
3. Who owns each one?
    
4. Who can access them?
    
5. Which vendors receive them?
    
6. Which AI tools may process them?
    
7. How long are they retained?
    
8. How are they deleted or archived?
    
9. How often is access reviewed?
    
10. What evidence proves our controls are working?
    

If leadership cannot answer these questions, data governance is not mature enough.
