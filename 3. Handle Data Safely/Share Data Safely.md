### Goal

For this subsection, the key control is **controlled sharing**: employees must know how to choose the right recipient, permission level, channel, and duration before data leaves its current location.

We include recipient verification, link permissions, external collaborators, public links, view-only access, expiration dates, shared folders, and misdirected emails.

### Learning Objective

By the end of this section, we will understand how to share data, files, links, screenshots, documents, and records in a way that reduces the risk of accidental exposure, unauthorized access, or misuse.

Employees should learn this core behavior:

**Before sharing data, confirm the recipient, the purpose, the sensitivity, the permission level, and the approved sharing method.**

Managers and executives should learn this assurance principle:

**The company must define how sensitive information can be shared internally, externally, and through cloud tools.**

### Why This Matters

Data sharing is part of normal business. Employees share files with colleagues, customers, suppliers, vendors, consultants, auditors, and partners every day.

The risk is that data can be shared too broadly, sent to the wrong person, exposed through a public link, forwarded in an email thread, uploaded to the wrong platform, or left accessible long after the business need ends.

**Data-sharing mistakes can lead to:**

- Customer information exposure
    
- Employee information exposure
    
- Contract or legal document leaks
    
- Financial fraud
    
- Supplier or pricing disclosure
    
- Intellectual property loss
    
- Regulatory issues
    
- Reputational damage
    
- Social engineering attacks
    
- Loss of client trust
    

Many data leaks are not dramatic technical breaches. They are ordinary sharing mistakes.

**Examples include:**

- Sending a spreadsheet to the wrong recipient
    
- Sharing a cloud folder with “anyone with the link”
    
- Giving edit access when view-only access was enough
    
- Forwarding an email thread containing confidential history
    
- Sending HR data through an informal channel
    
- Uploading company files to a personal cloud account
    
- Leaving external contractors in a shared folder after the project ends
    
- Sharing screenshots that reveal sensitive information in the background
    

The goal is not to stop collaboration. The goal is to make sharing intentional and controlled.

### Core Rule

Before sharing any data, ask:

**Who needs this, why do they need it, what exactly do they need, and what is the safest approved way to share it?**

If the answer is unclear, stop and verify before sending.

### The Safe Sharing Decision Process

Employees should use a simple five-step process before sharing data.

#### Step 1: Confirm the Recipient

**Ask:**

- Am I sending this to the correct person?
    
- Is the recipient internal or external?
    
- Is the email address correct?
    
- Is autocomplete selecting the wrong person?
    
- Is this a distribution list?
    
- Does every recipient need this information?
    
- Is the external domain correct?
    
- Is the recipient approved to receive this data?
    

**Common mistake:**

An employee types a first name into email, selects the wrong person from autocomplete, and sends a sensitive file to the wrong recipient.

**Safe behavior:**

Check the full email address before sending sensitive information.

#### Step 2: Confirm the Purpose

**Ask:**

- Why is this information being shared?
    
- What task does it support?
    
- Is the request expected?
    
- Does the recipient have a valid business need?
    
- Is this part of an approved process?
    
- Is this request unusual, urgent, or secretive?
    

If the request involves money, credentials, HR data, legal documents, customer records, vendor changes, payroll, or security information, treat it as high-risk and verify separately.

#### Step 3: Confirm the Data Sensitivity

**Ask:**

- Does this contain personal information?
    
- Does this contain customer data?
    
- Does this contain employee or HR data?
    
- Does this contain financial information?
    
- Does this contain legal or contract information?
    
- Does this contain security information?
    
- Does this contain confidential business strategy?
    
- Does this contain credentials, keys, tokens, or recovery codes?
    

If yes, use stronger controls.

#### Step 4: Confirm the Permission Level

**Ask:**

- Does the recipient need view, comment, edit, download, or owner access?
    
- Can this be view-only?
    
- Can download be restricted?
    
- Can access expire?
    
- Can access be limited to named users?
    
- Should external forwarding or resharing be restricted?
    
- Should the file be password-protected or encrypted?
    

Use the lowest permission that allows the task to be completed.

#### Step 5: Confirm the Approved Sharing Method

**Ask:**

- Is email appropriate?
    
- Should this be shared through the company’s approved cloud platform?
    
- Should this be shared through a secure portal?
    
- Should this go through the CRM, HR, finance, or legal system?
    
- Should IT, legal, compliance, or management approve the sharing?
    
- Is this tool approved for sensitive data?
    
- Is this going to a personal account or unapproved platform?
    

Do not use personal email, personal cloud storage, or unapproved file-transfer tools for company information.

### Sharing Methods Considerations

#### Sharing by Email

Email is convenient, but it is also one of the easiest ways to send data to the wrong place.

**Before emailing sensitive information:**

- Check every recipient carefully.
    
- Avoid unnecessary CC or BCC recipients.
    
- Avoid sending sensitive information to distribution lists unless approved.
    
- Remove unnecessary attachments.
    
- Review long email threads before forwarding.
    
- Confirm whether the recipient is internal or external.
    
- Use approved encryption or secure email features where required.
    
- Avoid sending passwords, MFA codes, recovery codes, or API keys by email.
    

Email is often poor for highly sensitive information because once sent, it can be forwarded, downloaded, copied, or stored outside your control.

#### Sharing Through Cloud Links

Cloud sharing can be safer than attachments if configured correctly. It can also be dangerous if permissions are too broad.

**Before sharing a cloud link:**

- Share with named users where possible.
    
- Avoid “anyone with the link” unless explicitly approved.
    
- Use view-only access unless editing is required.
    
- Set an expiration date when available.
    
- Restrict downloads for sensitive files where possible.
    
- Avoid sharing entire folders when one file is enough.
    
- Check whether external sharing is allowed.
    
- Remove access when the task is complete.
    
- Review external users in shared folders.
    

Cloud sharing gives control only if permissions are used correctly.

#### Sharing Attachments

Attachments create copies. Once an attachment leaves your control, it may be forwarded, saved, uploaded, or exposed.

**Before sending an attachment:**

- Confirm that an attachment is necessary.
    
- Consider using controlled cloud access instead.
    
- Remove unnecessary data from the file.
    
- Check hidden tabs, comments, metadata, and tracked changes.
    
- Confirm the file version is correct.
    
- Confirm the recipient is correct.
    
- Use approved protection for sensitive documents.
    

Attachments are especially risky when they contain spreadsheets, HR records, customer exports, contracts, legal files, financial reports, or security information.

#### Sharing Spreadsheets

Spreadsheets are one of the highest-risk file types because they often contain hidden or forgotten data.

**Before sharing a spreadsheet:**

- Check all tabs.
    
- Check hidden tabs.
    
- Check hidden rows and columns.
    
- Remove unnecessary formulas.
    
- Remove comments and notes.
    
- Remove customer, employee, or financial fields that are not needed.
    
- Check filters to make sure hidden data is not still included.
    
- Check file properties and metadata.
    
- Save a reduced version if the recipient only needs part of the data.
    

Do not send the full spreadsheet when a reduced version will do.

#### Sharing Screenshots

Screenshots often reveal more than intended.

**Before sharing a screenshot:**

- Check open tabs.
    
- Check visible names, emails, account numbers, and customer details.
    
- Check chat windows or notifications.
    
- Check financial figures.
    
- Check internal comments.
    
- Crop the screenshot if possible.
    
- Blur or redact sensitive information.
    
- Avoid screenshots of dashboards containing sensitive data unless approved.
    

A screenshot is a document. Treat it like one.

#### Sharing With External Parties

External sharing requires extra caution.

**External parties may include:**

- Customers
    
- Vendors
    
- Suppliers
    
- Consultants
    
- Contractors
    
- Auditors
    
- Lawyers
    
- Agencies
    
- Partners
    
- Managed service providers
    
- Software providers
    

**Before sharing externally, ask:**

- Is the external party approved?
    
- Is there a contract or agreement in place?
    
- What exact information do they need?
    
- Is the data sensitive?
    
- Can the data be reduced, redacted, masked, or summarized?
    
- Should access expire?
    
- Who owns the relationship?
    
- Who approved the sharing?
    
- How will the external party protect the data?
    
- How will the data be deleted or returned when no longer needed?
    

External sharing should not be casual.

#### Sharing with Internal Teams

Internal sharing still requires control.

Do not assume that everyone inside the company should access all company information.

**Examples:**

- HR files should not be broadly available.
    
- Finance records should not be shared with unrelated teams.
    
- Legal documents should not be forwarded casually.
    
- Customer exports should not be placed in open folders.
    
- Security reports should not be shared beyond those who need them.
    
- Executive materials should be restricted to approved recipients.
    

“Internal” does not mean “open to everyone.”

#### Sharing Data Through AI Tools

AI tools can create a data-sharing risk because the user may paste or upload information into a third-party system.

**Before using an AI tool with company information:**

- Confirm the tool is approved.
    
- Confirm the type of data is allowed.
    
- Remove personal information where possible.
    
- Remove customer identifiers where possible.
    
- Remove credentials, API keys, or security information.
    
- Avoid uploading confidential documents unless approved.
    
- Use generic examples when possible.
    
- Do not paste legal, HR, financial, customer, or security data into public AI tools without approval.
    

A prompt can be a disclosure.

#### Sharing Data Through Chat Tools

Chat tools are often treated casually, but they may contain sensitive information.

**Be cautious before sending:**

- Passwords
    
- MFA codes
    
- Customer data
    
- HR information
    
- Legal documents
    
- Financial reports
    
- Screenshots
    
- Internal strategy
    
- Contract terms
    
- Security information
    
- Large file exports
    

Chat messages may be searchable, retained, screenshotted, forwarded, or integrated with other tools.

Use approved channels and avoid treating chat as a safe dumping ground for sensitive information.

#### Sharing Through Personal Accounts

Employees should not move company information into personal accounts.

**Avoid:**

- Personal email
    
- Personal cloud storage
    
- Personal messaging apps
    
- Personal AI accounts
    
- Personal file-transfer tools
    
- Personal USB drives
    
- Personal note-taking apps
    
- Personal screenshots or photo storage
    

This matters because personal accounts may not have company security controls, retention rules, access logs, contractual protections, or incident response coverage.

### What Employees Should Do

**Employees should:**

1. Confirm the recipient before sending.
    
2. Confirm the business purpose.
    
3. Check whether the information is sensitive.
    
4. Use approved company systems.
    
5. Use the lowest permission level required.
    
6. Share with named users instead of public links.
    
7. Use view-only access when editing is not required.
    
8. Set expiration dates where available.
    
9. Remove unnecessary data before sharing.
    
10. Check spreadsheets, screenshots, attachments, and email threads before sending.
    
11. Verify high-risk requests through a separate trusted channel.
    
12. Remove access when the task or project ends.
    
13. Report accidental sharing immediately.
    
14. Ask for help if unsure whether data can be shared.
    

### What Employees Should Not Do

Employees should not:

- Share sensitive data with “anyone with the link” unless approved
    
- Send sensitive files to personal email accounts
    
- Use personal cloud storage for work files
    
- Forward long email threads without checking them
    
- Share entire folders when one file is enough
    
- Give edit access when view-only is enough
    
- Leave external users in shared folders indefinitely
    
- Send passwords, MFA codes, recovery codes, or API keys
    
- Upload confidential data to unapproved AI tools
    
- Share screenshots without reviewing what is visible
    
- Ignore autocomplete mistakes in email
    
- Assume external recipients can be trusted without approval
    
- Delay reporting if data was sent to the wrong person
    

### If You Share Data by Mistake

Mistakes must be reported quickly.

If you send data to the wrong person, share the wrong file, expose a public link, upload sensitive information to the wrong tool, or give access too broadly:

1. Stop further sharing.
    
2. Do not try to hide the mistake.
    
3. Report it immediately through the company’s approved process.
    
4. Provide the facts: what was shared, with whom, when, how, and what type of data was involved.
    
5. Follow instructions from IT, security, legal, HR, or management.
    
6. Do not delete evidence unless instructed.
    

Fast reporting can reduce damage.

### Manager Responsibilities

Managers should make safe sharing normal in daily work.

**Managers should:**

- Define which data their team can share and with whom.
    
- Make sure employees know approved sharing tools.
    
- Review high-risk external sharing.
    
- Challenge broad permissions.
    
- Encourage view-only access where appropriate.
    
- Make sure contractors and vendors receive only the information they need.
    
- Ensure external access is removed after projects end.
    
- Discourage personal email or personal cloud storage.
    
- Ensure staff know how to report accidental sharing.
    
- Review team folders for excessive access.
    
- Support employees who slow down before sharing sensitive information.
    

Managers should not ask employees to bypass approved sharing methods for convenience.

### Executive and Key Staff Assurance Points

Executives and key staff should verify that the company has clear and enforceable data-sharing controls.

Leadership should ensure the company has:

- Approved tools for file sharing
    
- Approved tools for secure external collaboration
    
- Rules for email attachments
    
- Rules for cloud links
    
- Rules for external sharing
    
- Rules for AI tool use
    
- Rules for personal account use
    
- Data classification guidance
    
- Access expiration or review processes
    
- External user review processes
    
- Monitoring of public or external sharing where possible
    
- Incident reporting procedures for accidental disclosure
    
- Contractual controls for vendors receiving sensitive information
    
- Training for departments that handle sensitive data
    

The leadership question is:

**Can sensitive information be shared safely, and can we detect when it is shared unsafely?**

### Warning Signs of Unsafe Data Sharing

The company may have unsafe sharing practices if:

- Employees use public links by default
    
- Sensitive files are emailed as attachments without review
    
- External users remain in folders after projects end
    
- Personal email is used for work files
    
- Teams use personal cloud storage for convenience
    
- No one reviews shared folders
    
- Employees do not understand view, edit, owner, and download permissions
    
- AI tools are used without data rules
    
- Contractors receive more data than needed
    
- Employees do not report accidental sharing
    
- Sensitive spreadsheets are sent without checking hidden tabs
    
- Long email threads are forwarded externally without review
    
- Managers ask staff to “just send everything”
    

These are not small mistakes. They are common paths to data exposure.


### Examples


#### Example: Wrong Recipient

An employee sends a customer pricing file to “Alex” using email autocomplete. The file goes to the wrong Alex, who is external.

**Unsafe response:** - Ignore it and hope the recipient deletes it.

**Safe response:** - Report immediately. Provide details about what was sent, who received it, when it was sent, and whether the file contained sensitive information.

#### Example: Public Cloud Link

A project folder is shared using “anyone with the link” because it is easier for external contractors.

The folder contains contracts, customer notes, pricing, and internal timelines.

**Risk:** - Anyone who receives or discovers the link may access the folder.

**Safer approach:** - Share only the required files with named users, use view-only access where appropriate, set expiration dates, and remove access after the project ends.

#### Example: Forwarded Email Thread

An employee forwards a long email thread to a vendor. The latest message asks a simple scheduling question, but earlier messages include internal pricing and legal comments.

**Risk:** - Sensitive internal information is disclosed.

**Safer approach:** - Start a new email containing only the scheduling question.

#### Example: Screenshot Leak

An employee sends a screenshot to a software vendor to report an error. The screenshot shows customer names, account numbers, and internal chat notifications.

**Risk:** - Unnecessary sensitive information is disclosed to the vendor.

**Safer approach:** - Crop, blur, or recreate the screenshot with only the error visible.

#### Example: AI Upload

An employee uploads a confidential contract to an unapproved public AI tool and asks for a summary.

**Risk:** - Sensitive legal and commercial information may be exposed to an unapproved service.

**Safer approach:** - Use an approved tool and follow company rules. Remove sensitive details if possible.


### Key Takeaway

Sharing data safely means making deliberate choices before information moves.

Employees should confirm the recipient, purpose, sensitivity, permission level, and approved sharing method before sending or granting access. Managers should reinforce safe sharing habits and review risky sharing practices. Executives should verify that the company has approved tools, external sharing controls, AI rules, and reporting procedures for accidental disclosure.

**Data sharing is not just communication. It is access control.**
