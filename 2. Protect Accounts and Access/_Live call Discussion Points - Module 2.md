### Questions Leaders Should Ask

Executives and key staff should ask:

1. Do we have an approved password manager?
    
2. What percentage of staff are using it?
    
3. Are work passwords unique and separate from personal passwords?
    
4. Is MFA enabled for email, cloud platforms, finance systems, HR systems, and administrator accounts?
    
5. Do any departments still use shared passwords?
    
6. Are passwords stored in spreadsheets, documents, or shared folders?
    
7. Are password reset procedures secure?
    
8. Are former employees removed from systems quickly?
    
9. Are privileged accounts protected more strongly than standard accounts?
    
10. Are staff trained to report password manager alerts, suspicious login prompts, or unexpected password reset emails?
    



### What Employees Should Not Do

**Employees should not:**

- Use the same password for multiple accounts
    
- Share passwords with coworkers
    
- Ask another employee for their password
    
- Store passwords in notes, documents, spreadsheets, or screenshots
    
- Save passwords in browsers on shared devices or in unsecured browsers
    
- Use personal email accounts to recover work accounts
    
- Use family-shared password vaults for work passwords
    
- Type passwords into unexpected login pages
    
- Enter passwords after clicking unexpected email links
    
- Ignore password manager breach warnings
    
- Create “easy to remember” passwords based on company or personal information
    



### MFA Questions Leaders Should Ask

Executives and key staff should ask:

1. Which systems require MFA today?
    
2. Which systems do not yet require MFA?
    
3. Is MFA mandatory for email, cloud storage, remote access, administrators, and finance systems?
    
4. What percentage of users are enrolled in MFA?
    
5. Are executives and administrators using stronger MFA?
    
6. Are push notifications protected with number matching?
    
7. Are SMS codes still used for sensitive systems?
    
8. Is phishing-resistant MFA available for high-risk users?
    
9. How are MFA reset requests verified?
    
10. Are repeated MFA denials or failed login attempts monitored?
    
11. Are employees trained to report unexpected prompts?
    
12. Are MFA exceptions reviewed and removed?
    

### MFA Evidence Leaders Should Request

Useful evidence includes:

- MFA enrollment report
    
- MFA coverage report by system
    
- List of systems without MFA
    
- List of accounts exempted from MFA
    
- Administrator and privileged-account MFA report
    
- MFA reset procedure
    
- Lost-device recovery procedure
    
- Conditional access or login-risk policy summary
    
- Logs of denied or repeated MFA prompts
    
- Records of MFA-related incidents or near misses
    
- Training completion records
    
- Roadmap for phishing-resistant MFA adoption
    

Leadership should not accept “we have MFA” as enough. The real question is:

**Where is MFA enforced, how strong is it, and can attackers still bypass it through people or weak recovery processes?**

### Never Share Accounts:

### Executive and Key Staff Assurance Points

Executives and key staff should verify that account sharing is not quietly embedded into business operations.

Leadership should ask whether the company has:

- Individual accounts for staff
    
- Individual accounts for contractors
    
- Named administrator accounts
    
- A process for requesting access
    
- A process for approving access
    
- A process for removing access
    
- A list of shared accounts
    
- A list of privileged accounts
    
- Controls for emergency accounts
    
- Delegated access for executive assistants
    
- Shared mailbox access without shared passwords
    
- Secure password manager sharing where credential sharing is unavoidable
    
- Monitoring for unusual login behavior
    
- Periodic access reviews
    

The executive question is not only:

“Do we have a password policy?”

The stronger question is:

“Can we prove who accessed what, when, and why?”
