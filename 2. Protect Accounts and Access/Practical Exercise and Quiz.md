### Discussion Scenario

An employee tells their manager:

“I use the same password for a few work tools because it is easier. They are not important systems, and I have MFA on my email anyway.”

Ask learners:

1. What is risky about this behavior?
    
2. Why does password reuse create business risk?
    
3. What should the employee do instead?
    
4. What should the manager do?
    
5. What should leadership verify across the company?
    

Expected answer:

Password reuse is risky because a breach of one account can expose other accounts. The employee should use the approved password manager to create unique passwords for every work account. The manager should reinforce password manager use and discourage reuse. Leadership should verify password manager adoption, MFA coverage, and whether shared or reused credentials are present in business workflows.

### Practical Exercise

Ask learners to classify each behavior as safe or unsafe.

#### Behavior 1

Using a password manager to generate a unique password for a new work application.

Expected answer:

Safe.

#### Behavior 2

Saving a work password in a spreadsheet called “Team Logins.”

Expected answer:

Unsafe.

#### Behavior 3

Using the same password for work email and a personal shopping account.

Expected answer:

Unsafe.

#### Behavior 4

Using a company-approved password manager protected by MFA.

Expected answer:

Safe.

#### Behavior 5

Sending a password to a coworker through chat because they need quick access.

Expected answer:

Unsafe.

### MFA


### Discussion Scenario

An employee receives five MFA approval prompts within two minutes. The employee is not trying to log in.

A few minutes later, someone calls and says:

“This is IT. We are fixing your account. Please approve the next prompt so we can complete the reset.”

Ask learners:

1. What is suspicious about this situation?
    
2. What should the employee avoid doing?
    
3. What should the employee do next?
    
4. What should the company investigate?
    
5. What should leadership verify about MFA configuration?
    

Expected answer:

The repeated prompts suggest a possible MFA fatigue attack. The phone call may be part of the social engineering attempt. The employee should not approve the prompt or share any codes. They should deny the prompt, end the call, and report the incident through the company’s official channel. The company should investigate whether the password was compromised, whether the login attempt came from an unusual location, and whether number matching or stronger MFA should be enforced.

### Practical Exercise

Classify each action as safe or unsafe.

#### Action 1

You approve an MFA prompt because you just typed your password into the company login page.

Expected answer:

Safe, assuming the login page was reached through a trusted route.

#### Action 2

You receive an MFA prompt while eating lunch and approve it because it is probably nothing.

Expected answer:

Unsafe.

#### Action 3

Someone from “IT” calls and asks you to read out your MFA code.

Expected answer:

Unsafe.

#### Action 4

You deny an unexpected MFA request and report it.

Expected answer:

Safe.

#### Action 5

You receive repeated MFA prompts and approve one to stop the notifications.

Expected answer:

Unsafe.

#### Action 6

Your company gives you a passkey or security key for a high-risk account, and you use it according to company instructions.

Expected answer:

Safe.

### Short Knowledge Check

**Question 1:** When should you approve an MFA prompt?

A. Whenever it appears  
B. Only when you personally started the login  
C. When someone claiming to be IT asks you to  
D. When it appears multiple times

**Correct answer:** B

**Question 2:** What should you do if you receive an MFA prompt you did not initiate?

A. Approve it quickly  
B. Ignore it forever  
C. Deny it and report it  
D. Send the code to your manager

**Correct answer:** C

**Question 3:** What is MFA fatigue or push bombing?

A. Forgetting your password  
B. Receiving repeated MFA prompts until you approve one by mistake or frustration  
C. Using a password manager too often  
D. Logging in from a new device

**Correct answer:** B

**Question 4:** Who is allowed to ask for your MFA code?

A. Your manager  
B. IT support  
C. A senior executive  
D. No one

**Correct answer:** D

**Question 5:** What is one benefit of phishing-resistant MFA, such as passkeys or security keys?

A. It removes the need for any security policies  
B. It is designed to reduce the risk of authenticating to fake login pages  
C. It lets employees share accounts safely  
D. It allows users to ignore suspicious prompts

**Correct answer:** B

### Never Share MFA Codes and Access


### Discussion Scenario

A small finance team uses one shared login for a vendor payment portal. The password is stored in a spreadsheet called “Finance Access.” Three current employees and one former employee know the password.

Ask learners:

1. What are the risks?
    
2. What could happen if the account is compromised?
    
3. Why is offboarding difficult in this situation?
    
4. What should the team do instead?
    
5. What should leadership verify?
    

Expected answer:

The shared login creates poor accountability, unsafe password storage, and offboarding risk. If compromised, an attacker may access payment data or change vendor information. The team should move to individual accounts, role-based permissions, and approved access management. Leadership should request an inventory of shared accounts and ensure there is a plan to remove or control them.

### Practical Exercise

Classify each behavior as safe or unsafe.

#### Behavior 1

An employee gives a coworker their password so the coworker can finish a task while they are away.

Expected answer:

Unsafe.

#### Behavior 2

A manager grants an employee access to a shared mailbox through the company’s normal permission system.

Expected answer:

Safe.

#### Behavior 3

An executive assistant logs into the executive’s email account using the executive’s password.

Expected answer:

Unsafe.

#### Behavior 4

A team uses a password manager’s approved shared-vault feature for a legacy account that cannot support individual users.

Expected answer:

Potentially safe, if approved, limited, logged, and reviewed.

#### Behavior 5

An employee approves an MFA prompt for a coworker who says they are locked out.

Expected answer:

Unsafe.

#### Behavior 6

An employee refuses to share credentials and helps the coworker request proper access.

Expected answer:

Safe.

### Short Knowledge Check

**Question 1:** Why is sharing an account dangerous?

A. It makes passwords longer  
B. It makes it harder to know who performed an action  
C. It improves teamwork  
D. It reduces the need for MFA

**Correct answer:** B

**Question 2:** What should you do if a coworker asks for your password?

A. Send it if you trust them  
B. Share it only through chat  
C. Refuse and help them request proper access  
D. Send it, then change it next month

**Correct answer:** C

**Question 3:** Which of the following should never be shared?

A. MFA code  
B. Password  
C. Security key  
D. All of the above

**Correct answer:** D

**Question 4:** What is the safer alternative to giving an assistant an executive’s password?

A. Let the assistant use the executive’s laptop  
B. Use approved delegated access  
C. Store the password in a private spreadsheet  
D. Remove MFA from the executive account

**Correct answer:** B

**Question 5:** What should leaders verify?

A. Whether shared accounts exist and how they are controlled  
B. Whether employees can remember passwords  
C. Whether teams prefer fast access  
D. Whether shared passwords are easy to find

**Correct answer:** A

Expected answer:

Safe.

Unsafe.

Unsafe.

A manager reviews team access and removes permissions that are no longer needed.

Safe.

**Question 2:** Why is excessive access dangerous?

C. It prevents people from working  
D. It only affects IT staff

**Correct answer:** B

**Question 3:** What should you do if you still have access to a system from an old role?

A. Keep it in case you need it later  
B. Ignore it

### Manage the Full Account Lifecycle



### Practical Exercise

Ask learners to classify each situation as safe or unsafe.

#### Situation 1

A new employee receives access based on a defined role profile and manager approval.

Expected answer:

Safe.

#### Situation 2

A new employee receives the same access as a long-serving employee without review.

Expected answer:

Unsafe.

#### Situation 3

A contractor’s access automatically expires at the end of the project unless renewed.

Expected answer:

Safe.

#### Situation 4

A former employee still appears in the user list for a cloud storage platform.

Expected answer:

Unsafe.

#### Situation 5

A manager reviews team access after a department restructure and removes unnecessary permissions.

Expected answer:

Safe.

#### Situation 6

An employee keeps admin access after finishing a temporary system migration project.

Expected answer:

Unsafe.

### Manager and Executive Exercise

Ask managers and executives to answer:

1. What systems must be included in offboarding?
    
2. Who tells IT when someone leaves?
    
3. Who tells IT when someone changes roles?
    
4. How are contractor end dates tracked?
    
5. How often are access reviews performed?
    
6. Which systems contain the most sensitive data?
    
7. Which roles have administrator access?
    
8. How do we know old access is actually removed?
    
9. What evidence can we produce from the last five departures?
    
10. What evidence can we produce from the last five role changes?
    

This exercise exposes whether account lifecycle management is real or assumed.

### Short Knowledge Check

**Question 1:** What are the three main account lifecycle events?

A. Click, open, reply  
B. Joiner, mover, leaver  
C. Password, code, device  
D. Email, browser, phone

**Correct answer:** B

**Question 2:** What is access creep?

A. When employees forget their passwords  
B. When people accumulate unnecessary access over time  
C. When MFA prompts appear repeatedly  
D. When a system runs slowly

**Correct answer:** B

**Question 3:** What should happen when an employee changes roles?

A. They should keep all old access just in case  
B. They should receive new access and have old access reviewed  
C. Their MFA should be removed  
D. Their account should be shared with their replacement

**Correct answer:** B

**Question 4:** Why are dormant accounts risky?

A. They are easier for employees to remember  
B. They may be forgotten and abused without detection  
C. They improve productivity  
D. They remove the need for access reviews

**Correct answer:** B

**Question 5:** What should leadership verify?

A. Whether account creation, role changes, reviews, and removals are documented and working  
B. Whether employees can create their own accounts  
C. Whether former employees can still access project files  
D. Whether contractors keep access indefinitely

**Correct answer:** A
