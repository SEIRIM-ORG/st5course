In this module we explore what employees should do with file sharing, apps, folders, systems, and third-party access, plus what leadership must verify through access reviews and offboarding.

### Key term: Least Privilege

Least privilege is defined as restricting users or processes to the minimum access needed to perform assigned tasks, and as staff we want to avoid requesting or holding on to access “just in case,”. This extra access increases the overall risk and the best policy is to always grant the minimum necessary file permissions, review external collaborators, and remove access when it is no longer required.

### Learning Objective

By the end of this section, lwe all will understand why access should be limited to what a person needs for their role, how excessive access creates business risk, and how employees, managers, and executives can help ensure access is granted, used, reviewed, and removed properly.

**Employees should learn this core behavior:** - Only request, use, and share the access needed for the task.

**Managers and executives should learn this assurance principle:** - Access should be intentional, documented, reviewed, and removed when no longer needed.

### Why This Matters

Every account, folder, application, system, and shared file gives someone access to company information or business operations.

Access is necessary for work, but too much access creates risk.

**If an employee, contractor, vendor, or attacker has more access than needed, they may be able to:**

- View sensitive files
    
- Download confidential data
    
- Change records
    
- Approve transactions
    
- Delete information
    
- Share files externally
    
- Access customer or employee data
    
- Modify system settings
    
- Install unauthorized tools
    
- Continue accessing systems after leaving the company
    

The risk becomes worse if an account is compromised. An attacker who gains access to a low-privilege account may be limited. An attacker who gains access to an overprivileged account may cause serious damage.

Access should not be granted because it is convenient, faster, or “just in case.” It should be granted because it is needed for a specific business purpose.

### Core Rule

The safest access rule is:

**Give people the minimum access they need, for only as long as they need it.**

This means:

- Do not request broad access if limited access is enough.
    
- Do not grant access to entire folders when one file is enough.
    
- Do not keep access after a project ends.
    
- Do not approve access requests without understanding the business need.
    
- Do not give admin rights to users who do not need them.
    
- Do not leave former employees, contractors, or vendors with active access.
    

Access is not a favor. It is a business risk decision.

### What “Necessary Access” Means

Necessary access means the person has enough access to do their assigned work, but not more than that.

**For example:**

- A finance employee may need access to invoices but not HR records.
    
- A marketing contractor may need access to campaign files but not the whole company drive.
    
- A manager may need to view team documents but not administrator settings.
    
- An employee may need view-only access, not editing access.
    
- A vendor may need access for a project, but not after the project ends.
    
- A temporary employee may need short-term access that expires automatically.
    

The goal is not to block work. The goal is to prevent unnecessary exposure.

### Common Types of Access

Employees should understand that access is not limited to logging into software. Access can include many things.

#### 1. File and Folder Access

This includes shared drives, cloud folders, project folders, document libraries, and file-sharing links.

**Risk:** - A folder may contain more sensitive information than the employee realizes.

**Correct behavior:** - Share the smallest reasonable amount of information with the smallest reasonable audience.

#### 2. Application Access

This includes CRM systems, finance platforms, HR tools, ticketing systems, email marketing platforms, customer portals, and cloud tools.

**Risk:** - Applications may contain customer data, financial records, internal communications, or administrative functions.

**Correct behavior:** - Only request the application access needed for the role.

#### 3. Email and Mailbox Access

This includes shared mailboxes, delegated inbox access, calendars, and distribution lists.

**Risk:** - Email accounts often contain sensitive attachments, reset links, contracts, customer messages, and internal decisions.

**Correct behavior:** - Use approved mailbox delegation rather than shared passwords, and remove access when it is no longer needed.

#### 4. Administrative Access

This includes system administrator, cloud administrator, finance administrator, HR administrator, website administrator, or database administrator access.

**Risk:** - Administrative access can change settings, create users, delete information, disable protections, or expose sensitive data.

**Correct behavior:** - Admin rights should be rare, approved, monitored, and limited.

#### 5. External Sharing Access

This includes sharing files, folders, systems, or project spaces with vendors, contractors, customers, agencies, and partners.

**Risk:** - External users may retain access after the project ends or may forward information beyond the intended audience.

**Correct behavior:** - External access should be approved, limited, and reviewed.

#### 6. Third-Party App Access

This includes apps that connect to email, calendars, cloud storage, documents, CRM systems, or communication platforms.

**Risk:** - A third-party app may be able to read, modify, or export company data.

**Correct behavior:** - Only connect approved applications and report unexpected app-permission requests.

### What Employees Should Do

**Employees should:**

1. Request access only when there is a clear business need.
    
2. Ask for the lowest access level that allows the work to be completed.
    
3. Use view-only access when editing is not required.
    
4. Avoid requesting access “just in case.”
    
5. Avoid granting access to entire folders when one file is enough.
    
6. Review external recipients before sharing files.
    
7. Remove access when a project, task, or contractor relationship ends.
    
8. Report if they still have access to systems or data they no longer need.
    
9. Avoid using admin access for routine work.
    
10. Ask for proper access instead of borrowing someone else’s account.
    
11. Be cautious before approving third-party app access.
    
12. Report unusual access requests or unexpected permission changes.
    


### File Sharing Rule

Before sharing a file or folder, employees should ask:

1. Who exactly needs access?
    
2. Do they need view, comment, or edit access?
    
3. Do they need one file or the whole folder?
    
4. Should access expire?
    
5. Is the recipient internal or external?
    
6. Does the file contain sensitive information?
    
7. Is this the approved sharing method?
    

The safest default is:

**Share less. Grant lower permission. Remove access sooner.**

### Access Level Examples

#### View Access

Use when someone only needs to read or review information.

Example:

A colleague needs to review a policy document.

Better choice:

Grant view access, not edit access.

#### Comment Access

Use when someone needs to provide feedback but should not change the source document.

Example:

A manager wants feedback from several team members on a draft.

Better choice:

Grant comment access, not full editing rights.

#### Edit Access

Use when someone needs to make changes.

Example:

A project team member is responsible for updating a working document.

Better choice:

Grant edit access only to the people responsible for editing.

#### Owner or Administrator Access

Use only when someone truly needs to manage permissions, settings, or system configuration.

Example:

A department employee wants admin access to a business system because it is convenient.

Better choice:

Deny broad admin access unless the role genuinely requires it.

### Access and Role Changes

Access must change when a person’s role changes.

For example:

- A finance employee moving to sales may no longer need finance-system access.
    
- A manager changing departments may no longer need access to old team folders.
    
- A contractor completing a project should lose project access.
    
- An employee leaving the company should have access removed promptly.
    
- A temporary worker should have access that expires when the assignment ends.
    

Access should follow the person’s current role, not their historical responsibilities.

### Contractor and Vendor Access

Contractors and vendors often need access to company systems or files, but that access should be controlled.

For external users, the company should define:

- What they can access
    
- Why they need access
    
- Who approved the access
    
- How long the access should last
    
- Who owns the relationship
    
- When access will be reviewed or removed
    
- Whether they can download, edit, or share files
    
- Whether MFA is required
    
- Whether their activity is logged
    

External access should not be left open indefinitely.

### Third-Party App Permissions

Many cloud tools ask for permission to connect to email, files, calendars, contacts, or messages.

**Employees should be cautious with permission requests such as:**

- “Read your email”
    
- “Send email as you”
    
- “View and manage your files”
    
- “Access your contacts”
    
- “Manage your calendar”
    
- “Read all documents in this workspace”
    
- “Maintain access to data you have given it access to”
    

These permissions can be powerful.

**Correct behavior:**

Do not approve third-party app access unless the tool is approved and the permission request makes sense for the work.

If unsure, stop and ask IT, security, or the system owner.

### Administrator Access

Administrator access should be treated as high-risk.

Admin access may allow a person to:

- Add or remove users
    
- Change permissions
    
- Disable security settings
    
- Access sensitive data
    
- Install software
    
- Delete records
    
- Modify system configuration
    
- Create new admin accounts
    
- Change billing, payment, or account settings
    

Employees should not ask for administrator rights because it is convenient. Managers should not approve admin rights casually.

Where possible, users should have separate normal and admin accounts. Routine work should be done using the normal account. Admin access should be used only when needed.

### Manager Responsibilities

Managers play a major role in access control because they understand what their team members actually need.

**Managers should:**

- Approve access only when there is a clear business need
    
- Avoid giving access “just in case”
    
- Match access to job responsibilities
    
- Ensure new employees receive the right access
    
- Ensure employees changing roles have old access removed
    
- Ensure contractors and temporary staff lose access when work ends
    
- Review team access periodically
    
- Avoid asking employees to share accounts or credentials
    
- Escalate access gaps instead of creating shortcuts
    
- Make sure high-risk access receives proper approval
    
- Encourage employees to report unnecessary access
    

Managers should not rubber-stamp access requests. Approval means accountability.

### Executive and Key Staff Assurance Points

Executives and key staff should verify that access is governed as a business control, not handled casually as an IT task.

**Leadership should ensure the company has:**

- A documented access request process
    
- Defined system owners
    
- Approval rules for sensitive systems
    
- Role-based access where practical
    
- Periodic access reviews
    
- A process for removing access when staff leave
    
- A process for changing access when staff change roles
    
- A contractor and vendor access process
    
- Controls for administrator and privileged accounts
    
- Monitoring of external sharing
    
- Review of third-party app permissions
    
- A process for documenting access exceptions
    

The executive focus should be simple:

**Who has access to what, why, who approved it, and when will it be reviewed?**


### Common Misconceptions

#### Misconception 1: “More access helps people work faster.”

Sometimes it does, but it also increases damage if the account is misused or compromised.

#### Misconception 2: “If someone is trustworthy, broad access is fine.”

Trust is not the issue. Access should match the role and business need.

#### Misconception 3: “Only IT is responsible for access.”

IT may configure access, but managers and system owners should know who needs access and why.

#### Misconception 4: “External sharing is safe if the link is hard to guess.”

Links can be forwarded, copied, exposed, or misconfigured. Sensitive files need controlled sharing.

#### Misconception 5: “Access can be cleaned up later.”

Old access is one of the easiest risks to ignore. Access should be removed when no longer needed.

### Key Takeaway

Access should be limited, intentional, and reviewed.

Employees should only request and use the access they need. Managers should approve access carefully and remove it when no longer required. Executives should verify that access to critical systems, files, vendors, contractors, and admin functions is governed properly.

**The more access an account has, the more damage it can cause if compromised.**
