### Goal

To ensure cybersecurity succeeds in your organization, note that it must be owned, reviewed, evidenced, and improved as a business function, not treated as a loose set of IT tasks. 

Further, cybersecurity performance goals can be positioned as practical baseline actions organizations can use to measure and improve cybersecurity posture.

### Learning Objective

Executives and key staff should understand how to turn cybersecurity training, policies, tools, and response plans into an operating discipline.

The core leadership behavior is:

**Assign ownership, review evidence, track risk, verify controls, and make sure cybersecurity work actually happens.**

The core assurance principle is:

**Cybersecurity is not operational because the company has training or tools. It is operational when responsibilities are assigned, controls are monitored, exceptions are tracked, providers are accountable, and leadership reviews progress.**

### Why This Matters

Many SMEs do not fail because they know nothing about cybersecurity. They fail because no one owns the follow-through.

**Common problems include:**

- Policies exist but are not enforced
    
- Employees are trained but managers do not reinforce behavior
    
- Security tools are installed but alerts are not reviewed
    
- Backups exist but restores are not tested
    
- MFA is planned but not fully deployed
    
- Vendors are trusted but not verified
    
- Vulnerabilities are scanned but not fixed
    
- Incident response plans exist but are not exercised
    
- Exceptions are allowed but never reviewed
    
- Executives receive verbal assurances but no evidence
    

Cybersecurity becomes reliable only when it is managed as part of normal business operations.

### Core Rule

The executive rule is:

**Do not manage cybersecurity by assumption. Manage it by ownership, evidence, and review.**

A weak assurance statement sounds like:

“IT says we are covered.”

A stronger assurance statement sounds like:

“We reviewed MFA coverage, backup restore test results, endpoint protection coverage, critical vulnerabilities, open incidents, vendor responsibilities, and overdue improvement actions this quarter.”

### What “Operational” Means

**Cybersecurity is operational when the company can answer these questions clearly:**

- Who owns cybersecurity risk?
    
- Who owns each major control?
    
- What systems and data are most important?
    
- What controls are in place?
    
- Which controls are missing or failing?
    
- Who reviews alerts?
    
- Who fixes vulnerabilities?
    
- Who manages third-party providers?
    
- Who approves exceptions?
    
- Who handles incidents?
    
- What evidence proves the work is happening?
    
- What risks remain open?
    
- What is improving over time?
    

If these questions cannot be answered, cybersecurity is still informal.

### 1. Assign Cybersecurity Ownership

Every company needs clear ownership.

This does not mean every SME needs a full internal cybersecurity department. It means the company must know who is responsible for decisions, oversight, and execution.

**At minimum, define:**

- Executive owner
    
- IT or security owner
    
- Incident response lead
    
- Data protection owner
    
- Vendor/MSP relationship owner
    
- Backup and recovery owner
    
- Access control owner
    
- Policy owner
    
- Department managers responsible for team behavior
    
- External provider responsibilities
    

One person may hold more than one role in a small company. That is acceptable. What is not acceptable is unclear ownership.

### 2. Define Decision Authority

Cybersecurity incidents and urgent risks require decisions.

**Leadership should define who can approve:**

- Emergency IT spending
    
- Disabling accounts
    
- Disconnecting systems
    
- Engaging external incident response support
    
- Contacting legal counsel
    
- Contacting cyber insurance
    
- Notifying customers or regulators
    
- Taking systems offline
    
- Restoring from backups
    
- Accepting a security exception
    
- Delaying a critical fix
    

Decision authority should be documented before pressure begins.

If the company waits for a crisis to decide who is in charge, response will be slower and weaker.

### 3. Monitor Security Alerts and Escalation

Security tools are useful only if someone reviews and acts on their warnings.

**Leadership should verify who monitors alerts from:**

- Endpoint protection
    
- Email security tools
    
- Identity and login systems
    
- MFA systems
    
- Cloud platforms
    
- Backup systems
    
- Vulnerability scanners
    
- Firewalls
    
- Remote-access systems
    
- Website or application security tools
    
- Managed detection and response providers
    
- Managed service providers
    

**The company should know:**

- Who receives alerts
    
- Which alerts are urgent
    
- How quickly alerts are reviewed
    
- Who escalates them
    
- What happens after hours
    
- How false positives are handled
    
- Whether serious alerts are tracked to closure
    

A dashboard is not enough. Someone must be accountable for action.

### 4. Verify Vendor, Cloud, and MSP Responsibilities

Many SMEs rely on external providers. That does not remove leadership responsibility.

The company should clearly define what each provider is responsible for.

**Examples:**

- Who manages MFA?
    
- Who manages backups?
    
- Who monitors endpoint alerts?
    
- Who reviews vulnerability scans?
    
- Who applies patches?
    
- Who manages firewall rules?
    
- Who manages cloud access?
    
- Who responds after hours?
    
- Who supports incident response?
    
- Who provides logs?
    
- Who confirms backup restore testing?
    
- Who reports security exceptions?
    
- Who offboards users?
    
- Who reviews third-party app access?
    

Do not accept “the provider handles security” as a complete answer.

A better standard is:

**What exactly does the provider handle, what evidence do they provide, and what remains our responsibility?**

### 5. Review Evidence, Not Just Promises

Executives do not need to perform technical work, but they should review evidence that controls are working.

**Useful evidence includes:**

- MFA coverage report
    
- Password manager adoption report
    
- Endpoint protection coverage report
    
- Device inventory
    
- Software inventory
    
- Patch compliance report
    
- Vulnerability scan and remediation report
    
- Critical vulnerability list
    
- Backup success and failure report
    
- Restore test results
    
- Incident and near-miss report
    
- Phishing report metrics
    
- Data exposure report
    
- Access review results
    
- Admin account review
    
- Vendor/MSP security reports
    
- Training completion records
    
- Tabletop exercise results
    
- Open risk and exception register
    
- Improvement action tracker
    

The goal is not to overwhelm leadership with technical detail. The goal is to show whether the company is improving or drifting.

### 6. Track Exceptions and Open Risks

Every company has exceptions.

**Examples:**

- A legacy system that cannot be patched
    
- A user who needs temporary admin rights
    
- A vendor without MFA support
    
- A system not covered by backups
    
- A device that cannot run endpoint protection
    
- A delayed vulnerability fix
    
- An external sharing exception
    
- A contractor needing temporary access
    
- A business tool not yet formally approved
    

Exceptions are not automatically wrong. Untracked exceptions are the problem.

**Each exception should include:**

- What the exception is
    
- Why it exists
    
- Who approved it
    
- What risk it creates
    
- What compensating control is in place
    
- When it expires
    
- Who owns closure
    
- When leadership will review it again
    

Permanent exceptions should be rare.

### 7. Convert Training Into Simple Company Rules

Training should become daily operating behavior.

**The company should maintain short, practical rules for:**

- Passwords and password managers
    
- MFA
    
- Account sharing
    
- Data handling
    
- File sharing
    
- Approved tools
    
- AI tool use
    
- Software installation
    
- Remote work
    
- Personal devices
    
- Lost devices
    
- Incident reporting
    
- Payment and vendor-change verification
    
- Backup storage locations
    

Rules should be simple enough for staff to follow under pressure.

A policy nobody reads will not protect the company.

A short rule reinforced by managers can.

### 8. Make Managers Part of the Control System

Managers are critical because they shape daily behavior.

**Managers should reinforce:**

- Reporting suspicious activity
    
- Verifying high-risk requests
    
- Using approved tools
    
- Storing files in approved systems
    
- Completing updates and restarts
    
- Not sharing accounts
    
- Not bypassing controls
    
- Reporting mistakes quickly
    
- Escalating incidents without delay
    

Managers should not create pressure that causes employees to ignore security rules.

If a manager says “just get it done,” employees may use unsafe shortcuts. Leadership must make clear that secure process is part of performance, not an obstacle to it.

### 9. Establish a Regular Cybersecurity Review Rhythm

Cybersecurity should be reviewed on a recurring schedule.

For many SMEs, a quarterly leadership review is practical.

**The review should cover:**

- Current major cyber risks
    
- Open incidents or near-misses
    
- Phishing and social engineering trends
    
- MFA and account security status
    
- Critical vulnerabilities and overdue remediation
    
- Patch and endpoint protection coverage
    
- Backup status and restore testing
    
- Access review results
    
- Vendor/MSP performance
    
- Open exceptions
    
- Training completion
    
- Policy updates
    
- Improvement actions
    
- Budget or resource needs
    

The review should result in decisions, not just discussion.

### 10. Maintain an Improvement Tracker

Findings should become actions.

**The company should track:**

- What needs to be fixed
    
- Who owns it
    
- Why it matters
    
- Priority level
    
- Due date
    
- Status
    
- Evidence of completion
    
- Executive escalation if overdue
    

**Improvement actions may come from:**

- Risk assessments
    
- Incidents
    
- Near-misses
    
- Tabletop exercises
    
- Vulnerability scans
    
- Backup tests
    
- Access reviews
    
- Vendor reviews
    
- Employee reports
    
- Audits
    
- Insurance reviews
    

The company should avoid discovering the same weakness repeatedly without fixing it.

### What Executives Should Do

Executives should:

- Assign clear ownership
    
- Ask for evidence
    
- Review risks regularly
    
- Fund necessary improvements
    
- Support secure processes
    
- Hold providers accountable
    
- Require incident response exercises
    
- Review backup and recovery capability
    
- Approve or reject high-risk exceptions
    
- Make managers accountable for secure team behavior
    
- Track improvement actions to closure
    
- Treat cybersecurity as business resilience
    

### What Executives Should Not Do

**Executives should not:**

- Assume IT has everything covered
    
- Treat cybersecurity as only a technical issue
    
- Accept verbal assurances without evidence
    
- Ignore overdue vulnerabilities
    
- Allow permanent exceptions without review
    
- Let vendors define accountability vaguely
    
- Skip incident response exercises
    
- Ignore backup restore testing
    
- Punish employees for fast reporting
    
- Let business pressure normalize unsafe shortcuts
    
- Review cybersecurity only after an incident
    

Leadership behavior sets the security culture.

### Manager Responsibilities

**Managers should:**

- Reinforce company security rules
    
- Make sure staff complete required training
    
- Support reporting without blame
    
- Escalate suspicious activity quickly
    
- Avoid pressuring staff into unsafe shortcuts
    
- Identify department-specific risks
    
- Keep team data in approved systems
    
- Ensure departing staff return devices and access
    
- Participate in exercises when relevant
    
- Close improvement actions assigned to their department
    

Managers turn policy into behavior.

### Employee Responsibilities

Employees should not be expected to manage cybersecurity operations.

**Their role is to:**

- Follow approved processes
    
- Use approved tools
    
- Protect accounts and devices
    
- Handle data safely
    
- Report suspicious activity
    
- Report mistakes quickly
    
- Cooperate with updates, remediation, and incident response
    
- Ask before bypassing controls
    

Employees are part of the control environment, but leadership owns the system.

### Questions Leaders Should Ask

**Executives and key staff should ask:**

1. Who owns cybersecurity risk?
    
2. Who owns day-to-day security execution?
    
3. What evidence shows our key controls are working?
    
4. Are alerts monitored and escalated?
    
5. Are critical vulnerabilities fixed on time?
    
6. Are backups protected and restore-tested?
    
7. Are incidents and near-misses reviewed?
    
8. Are vendors and MSPs providing useful security evidence?
    
9. Are access rights reviewed?
    
10. Are exceptions documented and time-limited?
    
11. Are employees using approved tools?
    
12. Are managers reinforcing security behavior?
    
13. Are tabletop exercises performed?
    
14. Are improvement actions tracked to closure?
    
15. What cyber risk requires an executive decision this quarter?
    

The strongest executive question is:

**What are we assuming that we have not verified?**

Evidence should be understandable to leadership and specific enough to drive decisions.

### Warning Signs Cybersecurity Is Not Operational

The company may not be operating cybersecurity effectively if:

- Nobody owns cyber risk at executive level
    
- IT owns everything but has no authority or budget
    
- The MSP is trusted but not measured
    
- Alerts are generated but not reviewed
    
- Backups exist but restores are not tested
    
- Vulnerabilities are reported but not fixed
    
- Incidents are handled informally
    
- Exceptions are undocumented
    
- Policies are long but unused
    
- Employees do not know how to report
    
- Managers override security rules for convenience
    
- Access reviews are skipped
    
- Security tools are installed but coverage is incomplete
    
- Leadership sees no metrics or evidence
    
- The same issues appear repeatedly without closure
    

These are not minor administrative problems. They are signs that cybersecurity is not embedded into the business.

### Key Takeaway

Cybersecurity becomes operational when it is owned, evidenced, reviewed, and improved.

Employees follow secure behaviors. Managers reinforce them. IT, security, and providers execute controls. Executives verify that the system works.

Training, tools, and policies are not enough by themselves.

**If no one owns it, no one reviews it, and no one tracks it to closure, it is not operational.**
