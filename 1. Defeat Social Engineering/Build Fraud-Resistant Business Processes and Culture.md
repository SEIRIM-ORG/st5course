Business managers and executives should understand that social engineering is not only an employee-awareness problem. It is also a business-process and company-culture problem.

Employees should know when to slow down and verify. Managers and executives should know how to build processes that make it difficult for fraud to succeed, even when a message looks convincing or appears to come from a trusted person.
<br>
<br>
### Why This Matters

Social engineering works because attackers exploit normal business behavior. They know employees want to be helpful, respond quickly, respect authority, avoid conflict, and solve problems.

**A weak process gives the attacker an opening.**

For example:

- If one person can change supplier bank details alone, attackers will target that person.
    
- If urgent executive requests are allowed to bypass normal approvals, attackers will impersonate executives.
    
- If employees are punished for slowing things down, attackers will use urgency.
    
- If staff are embarrassed to report mistakes, attackers gain more time.
    
- If finance, HR, procurement, and management teams are not trained for role-specific scams, attackers will target those functions.
    

The goal is not to make people suspicious of everything. The goal is to design business processes where important actions require verification, accountability, and documentation.
<br>
<br>
### Core Principle

A fraud-resistant company does not rely only on employees “spotting scams.”

It uses clear procedures, independent verification, approval controls, and a culture where employees are expected to pause when a request is risky.

**The core rule is:**

- **High-risk actions should never depend on one message, one person, or one rushed decision.**
<br>
<br>

### What Is a Fraud-Resistant Business Process?

A fraud-resistant business process is a normal workflow that includes safeguards against manipulation, impersonation, and rushed decision-making.

**It should make it difficult for an attacker to:**

- Redirect payments
    
- Change vendor banking details
    
- Modify payroll information
    
- Gain access to sensitive files
    
- Persuade employees to share credentials
    
- Bypass approval rules
    
- Pressure staff into secrecy
    
- Abuse executive authority
    
- Exploit confusion during busy periods
    
- Use compromised accounts to request dangerous actions
    

The process should protect employees from being forced to make risky decisions alone.
<br>
<br>
### Common Business Processes That Need Protection
<br>
<br>

#### 1. Payment Approval

Payment requests are a major target for social engineering.

**A strong payment process should include:**

- Clear payment approval limits
    
- Dual approval for significant payments
    
- Verification of unusual or urgent payment requests
    
- Separation between the person requesting payment and the person approving it
    
- Documentation of exceptions
    
- Extra checks for new vendors, foreign payments, or unusual amounts
    
- A rule that payment approval cannot be bypassed by urgency or seniority
    

**Example risk:**

An attacker impersonates the CEO and asks finance to send an urgent transfer before the end of the day.

**Fraud-resistant response:**

The payment must go through the same approval process as any other payment. Executive urgency does not remove verification requirements.
<br>
<br>
#### 2. Supplier and Vendor Bank-Detail Changes

Vendor bank-detail changes are especially dangerous because attackers can redirect legitimate payments to fraudulent accounts.

**A strong vendor-change process should include:**

- Verification using contact details already stored in approved vendor records
    
- No changes based only on email
    
- Dual review before banking details are updated
    
- Documentation of who requested, verified, approved, and completed the change
    
- Alerts or review for changes made shortly before a payment
    
- Periodic review of vendor master data
    

**Example risk:**

A supplier appears to email new banking details and requests that the next invoice be paid to the new account.

**Fraud-resistant response:**

The employee must call the supplier using the existing approved phone number, not the number in the email, and follow the company’s vendor-change procedure.
<br>
<br>
#### 3. Payroll and Employee Record Changes

Payroll changes are also a target. Attackers may try to redirect salaries, benefits, tax documents, or employee data.

**A strong payroll-change process should include:**

- Verification of direct deposit changes
    
- Employee confirmation through approved internal channels
    
- Restrictions on changes made only by email
    
- Manager or HR review for unusual changes
    
- Documentation of all changes
    
- Alerts for changes made close to payroll deadlines
    

**Example risk:**

An attacker impersonates an employee and asks HR to update direct deposit information before the next payroll run.

**Fraud-resistant response:**

HR verifies through the company’s approved employee channel before making the change.
<br>
<br>
#### 4. Sensitive Data Requests

Attackers may request customer lists, employee records, contracts, financial documents, board materials, or credentials.

**A strong data-request process should include:**

- Clear data classification
    
- Defined approval rules for sensitive information
    
- Verification of external recipients
    
- Use of approved secure file-sharing methods
    
- Restrictions on sending sensitive data to personal email accounts
    
- Review of unusual or urgent data requests
    
- Logging of sensitive exports where possible
    

**Example risk:**

A senior leader appears to ask an assistant to send a confidential employee file to a new external email address.

**Fraud-resistant response:**

The assistant verifies the request through a trusted internal channel and confirms whether the recipient is approved to receive the file.
<br>
<br>
#### 5. IT Help Desk and Account Recovery

Attackers often target help desks because they can reset passwords, add devices, or change access.

**A strong help-desk process should include:**

- Identity verification before password resets
    
- Extra checks for executives and privileged users
    
- Restrictions on MFA reset requests
    
- Logging and review of account recovery events
    
- Clear escalation for suspicious requests
    
- No password sharing under any circumstances
    

**Example risk:**

An attacker calls the help desk pretending to be an executive locked out before a meeting.

**Fraud-resistant response:**

The help desk follows identity verification procedures, even if the caller is impatient or senior.
<br>
<br>
#### 6. Executive and Board Communications

Executives are commonly impersonated because staff may hesitate to challenge them.

**A strong executive-communication process should include:**

- A rule that executives must follow the same verification procedures as everyone else
    
- Clear expectations that staff may challenge unusual requests
    
- Approved communication channels for sensitive instructions
    
- No “secret” payment or data requests outside normal workflows
    
- Training for executive assistants and finance staff
    
- Leadership modeling of safe behavior
    

**Example risk:**

An executive’s name is used to pressure an employee into completing a confidential transaction.

**Fraud-resistant response:**

The employee is expected to verify, and leadership supports the delay.
<br>
<br>
### Culture Matters as Much as Process

A written procedure is not enough if the company culture contradicts it.

**A company becomes vulnerable when employees believe:**

- Speed matters more than safety
    
- Senior people should not be questioned
    
- Reporting mistakes will get them punished
    
- Security is only IT’s responsibility
    
- It is better to quietly fix a mistake than report it
    
- Exceptions are normal during urgent work
    
- Verification is rude or unnecessary
    
- “Just this once” is acceptable
    

**A fraud-resistant culture teaches the opposite:**

- Verification is professional
    
- Reporting is responsible
    
- Procedures apply to everyone
    
- Urgency does not cancel controls
    
- Mistakes must be reported quickly
    
- Employees are allowed to pause
    
- Leaders must model secure behavior
    
<br>
<br>

### The Leadership Message Employees Need to Hear

Employees need clear permission to slow down when something is risky.

**Suggested leadership message:**

“Cybersecurity is part of how we protect the business. If a request involves payment, credentials, sensitive data, vendor changes, payroll, or unusual urgency, you are expected to verify it before acting. You will not be punished for slowing down to confirm a high-risk request. You will be supported for reporting concerns quickly.”

This message should come from leadership, not only from IT.
<br>
<br>

### What Executives Must Verify

Executives and key decision-makers should not simply ask, “Have we trained people?”

That question is too weak.

**They should ask:**

- Can one person approve a high-risk payment alone?
    
- Can vendor bank details be changed based only on email?
    
- Are payroll changes independently verified?
    
- Are staff allowed to challenge urgent executive requests?
    
- Are high-risk workflows documented?
    
- Are exceptions reviewed?
    
- Are phishing and fraud attempts reported to leadership?
    
- Are finance, HR, procurement, and executive assistants given role-specific training?
    
- Are incident reports used to improve procedures?
    
- Are external providers and managed service providers included in response procedures?
    

The objective is assurance. Leadership should verify that controls exist and are actually used.
<br>
<br>

### Example Scenario: The Urgent Supplier Payment

An accounts payable employee receives an email that appears to come from a regular supplier.

**The message says:**

“Please note that our banking details have changed. The attached invoice is due today. Please send payment to the new account below to avoid late fees.”

The email looks professional. The sender’s name is familiar. The invoice amount matches a real project.

A weak process would allow the employee to update the bank details and process payment based only on the email.

**A fraud-resistant process requires:**

1. Stop before changing the payment details.
    
2. Check whether the request is high-risk.
    
3. Verify using the vendor contact details already stored in approved records.
    
4. Require a second approval before changing banking information.
    
5. Document the verification and approval.
    
6. Report the message if it appears suspicious.
    

The point is not whether the email looks real. The point is that bank-detail changes are always high-risk.
<br>
<br>

### Example Scenario: The Executive Override

A manager receives a message that appears to come from the managing director.

**The message says:**

“I need you to send the employee compensation file to this external address before the meeting. Do not involve anyone else yet.”

This request uses authority, secrecy, and urgency.

**A weak culture tells the manager:**

“The managing director asked, so I should do it.”

**A fraud-resistant culture tells the manager:**

“This is a high-risk data request. I need to verify through a trusted internal channel and follow the data-sharing process.”

The manager should not be punished for slowing down. The manager should be supported for protecting the company.
<br>
<br>

### Example Scenario: The Help Desk Reset

A person calls the help desk claiming to be a senior executive who is locked out before a client call.

**The caller is frustrated and says:**

“I do not have time for all these questions. Just reset it now.”

A weak process allows the help desk to skip identity verification because the caller sounds senior.

A fraud-resistant process requires identity verification every time, especially for senior and privileged users.

The more powerful the account, the more important the verification.
<br>
<br>

### Minimum Controls to Build Into the Business

For SMEs, the minimum fraud-resistant controls should include:

- Dual approval for significant payments
    
- Independent verification for vendor bank-detail changes
    
- Independent verification for payroll changes
    
- A clear phishing and fraud reporting channel
    
- A rule that passwords and MFA codes are never shared
    
- Role-specific training for finance, HR, procurement, executives, and help desk staff
    
- A documented process for sensitive data requests
    
- A documented process for account recovery
    
- Review of exceptions and near-misses
    
- Leadership support for employees who pause and verify
    

These controls do not need to be complex. They need to be clear, followed, and enforced.
<br>
<br>

### Leadership Assurance Evidence

Executives and key staff should request evidence, not verbal reassurance.

**Useful evidence includes:**

- Payment approval policy
    
- Vendor-change procedure
    
- Payroll-change procedure
    
- Sensitive data-sharing procedure
    
- Help-desk identity verification procedure
    
- Training records for high-risk roles
    
- Fraud and phishing reporting metrics and results from phishing simulations
    
- Records of payment or vendor-change exceptions
    
- Incident or near-miss review notes


The goal is not paperwork for its own sake. The goal is to confirm that the company has working safeguards against social engineering.

<br>
<br>

### Leadership should ask:

- Is security awareness training provided during onboarding and periodically afterward?
    
- Can staff report suspicious messages with one simple action?
    
- Are phishing simulations used to identify training gaps?
    
- Are reporting rates measured, not only failure rates?
    
- Are finance, HR, procurement, executives, and help-desk staff given role-specific scenarios?
    
- Are employees encouraged to report mistakes immediately without fear?
    

**Evidence to request:** training completion reports, phishing-reporting metrics, verification procedures, payment-approval records, and examples of lessons learned from reported attempts.
<br>
<br>
### Key Takeaway

Social engineering succeeds when people, processes, and culture fail together.

A fraud-resistant company does not depend on every employee spotting every scam. It builds workflows that require verification for high-risk actions, supports employees who slow down, and ensures that leaders follow the same rules they expect from everyone else.

**Good culture makes secure behavior normal. Good process makes fraud harder to complete.**
