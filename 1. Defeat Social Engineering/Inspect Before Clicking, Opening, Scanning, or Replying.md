In this section we cover the practice to pause before interacting with a suspicious message, inspect the key warning signs, and choose the safest next action before clicking a link, opening an attachment, scanning a QR code, replying to a request, or entering login details.

### Why This Matters

Many social engineering attacks succeed because the victim reacts too quickly. The message may look ordinary: a document to review, an invoice to approve, a delivery update, a shared file, a password reset, or a request from a manager. The danger is not always obvious.

Attackers want the recipient to act before thinking. They may use urgency, authority, curiosity, fear, or convenience to push the person into clicking, opening, scanning, replying, approving, or logging in. 

**The goal of this section is to build these simple habits:**

- Pause before interacting
- Inspect the message
- Verify anything high-risk
- Report anything suspicious

### The Core Rule

Before you click a link, open an attachment, scan a QR code, reply to a request, or enter a password, ask:

- **Was I expecting this, does it make business sense, and can I verify it safely?**

If the answer is no, stop and check.
<br>
<br>

### What to Inspect
<br>
<br>

### 1. Inspect the Sender

Do not rely only on the display name. A message can show a familiar name while coming from an unfamiliar or slightly altered address.

Check for:

- Misspelled domains
    
- Personal email addresses used for company business
    
- Strange sender addresses
    
- Unexpected external senders
    
- A message that appears to come from someone senior but uses unusual wording
    
- A supplier or customer message that does not match the normal communication pattern
    

**Example warning sign:**

A message says it is from the finance director, but the email address is from a free email account or a domain that is slightly different from the company’s real domain.

**Correct action:**

Do not reply with sensitive information. Verify through a trusted internal channel.
<br>
<br>

### 2. Inspect the Link Before Clicking

Links can be disguised. The visible text may say one thing, but the destination may go somewhere else.

**Before clicking:**

- Hover over the link if using a desktop device.
    
- Check whether the domain is correct.
    
- Be cautious with shortened links.
    
- Do not trust a link only because the logo or formatting looks professional.
    
- When in doubt, go to the known website directly instead of using the link in the message.
    

**Example warning sign:**

A message says “View Microsoft 365 Document,” but the link points to an unfamiliar domain.

**Correct action:**

Do not click. Open Microsoft 365 directly through your normal bookmark or company portal.
<br>
<br>

### 3. Inspect Attachments Before Opening

Attachments are commonly used to deliver malware or trick users into entering credentials.

**Be cautious with:**

- Unexpected invoices
    
- Unknown file-sharing documents
    
- Compressed files
    
- Files asking you to enable macros
    
- Attachments with unusual file names
    
- Attachments from people who do not normally send you files
    
- Documents that ask you to log in again before viewing
    

**Example warning sign:**

You receive an invoice from a supplier you do not usually work with, and the message pressures you to open it immediately.

**Correct action:**

Do not open the attachment. Verify the invoice through the normal purchasing or finance process.
<br>
<br>

### 4. Inspect QR Codes Like Links

A QR code is just another way to send you to a website or trigger an action. It should be treated with the same caution as a suspicious link.

**Be cautious when a QR code appears in:**

- Unexpected emails
    
- Printed flyers in the office
    
- Delivery notices
    
- Parking or payment messages
    
- Fake authentication prompts
    
- Posters placed in public areas
    
- Messages asking you to scan to “restore access” or “confirm your account”
    

**Example warning sign:**

An email says your account will be locked unless you scan a QR code and log in.

**Correct action:**

Do not scan it. Go directly to the company’s official login page or report the message.
<br>
<br>

### 5. Inspect Login Requests

A common attack is to send a fake login page that looks like a real company system.

**Before entering credentials:**

- Check whether you reached the page through a trusted route.
    
- Check whether the web address is correct.
    
- Be suspicious if a document link asks you to log in again unexpectedly.
    
- Never enter your password into a page opened from an unexpected message.
    
- Never share MFA codes or approve login prompts you did not initiate.
    

**Example warning sign:**

You click a document link and are asked to log in, even though you are already signed into your company account.

**Correct action:**

Close the page and report the message.
<br>
<br>

### 6. Inspect the Request Itself

Even if the sender, link, or attachment looks normal, the request may still be suspicious.

**Treat the following as high-risk:**
<br>


- Requests for payment
    
- Requests to change bank details
    
- Requests for passwords or MFA codes
    
- Requests to send confidential files
    
- Requests to bypass normal approval steps
    
- Requests to keep something secret
    
- Requests from senior staff that are unusually urgent
    
- Requests involving payroll, vendors, legal documents, or customer data
    

**Example warning sign:**

A senior executive asks you to send a confidential file to an external email address and says not to discuss it with anyone.

**Correct action:**

Verify the request through a separate trusted channel before taking any action.
<br>
<br>

### The Five-Second Inspection Habit

Before interacting with a message, take five seconds and check:
<br>


1. **Sender:** Is this really who it claims to be?
    
2. **Context:** Was I expecting this?
    
3. **Link or attachment:** Does the destination or file make sense?
    
4. **Request:** Is it asking me to do something risky?
    
5. **Pressure:** Is it pushing urgency, secrecy, fear, or authority?
    

If anything feels wrong, stop and report it.
<br>
<br>

### What Not to Do

**Do not:**

- Click just to “see what happens”
    
- Open an attachment because the message looks professional
    
- Scan a QR code from an unexpected message
    
- Reply with sensitive information
    
- Enter credentials into a page opened from an unexpected link
    
- Approve an MFA request you did not start
    
- Use the phone number or contact details provided in the suspicious message
    
- Delete the message before reporting it
    
- Forward the message to coworkers for their opinion
    
<br>
<br>

### Correct Employee Action

If a message seems suspicious:

1. Stop interacting with it.
    
2. Do not click, open, scan, reply, or log in.
    
3. Report it using the company’s approved reporting method.
    
4. If the request appears to involve money, credentials, customer data, legal documents, or executive instructions, verify through a separate trusted channel.
    
5. If you already clicked, opened, scanned, replied, or entered information, report it immediately.
    
<br>
<br>

### Manager and Executive Assurance Points

Managers and executives should make sure employees are not relying on judgment alone. The company should support inspection with clear processes and technical controls.

**Leadership should verify that:**

- Employees know how to report suspicious messages.
    
- The company has a phishing-report button or simple reporting channel.
    
- High-risk requests require independent verification.
    
- Finance, HR, procurement, and executive assistants receive role-specific social engineering training.
    
- Email filtering, attachment scanning, and malicious-link protection are in place.
    
- Domain protection controls such as SPF, DKIM, and DMARC are configured where appropriate.
    
- Staff are trained not to approve unexpected MFA prompts.
    
- Employees are not punished for reporting mistakes quickly.
    
- Reported phishing attempts are reviewed for patterns and used to improve defenses.
    


### Takeaway

A suspicious message does not need to look obviously fake. The safest employees are not the ones who can spot every scam by appearance. They are the ones who pause, inspect, verify high-risk requests, and report quickly.
