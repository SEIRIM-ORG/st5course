## Goal

Staff should be able to identify high-risk requests, stop before acting, and verify the request through a trusted communication channel that is separate from the original message.
<br>
<br>

### Why This Matters

Many social engineering attacks do not rely on malware or technical tricks. They succeed because the attacker makes a request that appears normal, urgent, or authoritative.

The message may look like it came from a senior executive, manager, supplier, customer, bank, IT support team, or trusted platform. It may use correct branding, professional language, and familiar business details. In some cases, the attacker may even use a real compromised email account.

That is why checking only the message itself is not enough.

**For high-risk requests, the safest rule is:**

- **Do not verify the request inside the same message thread. Verify it through a separate trusted channel.**
<br>
<br>

### Core Rule

If a request involves money, credentials, sensitive data, account access, legal documents, vendor changes, payroll, or unusual urgency, stop and verify it separately before acting.

A separate channel means using a known and trusted method that does not come from the suspicious message itself.

**Examples include:**

- Calling a known phone number already saved in company records
    
- Using the company directory
    
- Contacting the person through an approved internal chat tool
    
- Confirming through an existing vendor portal
    
- Asking through the company’s ticketing or approval system
    
- Checking with a manager through an established internal process
    

Do not use the phone number, email address, link, QR code, or contact details included in the suspicious message.

But remain suspicious, other channels may also be compromised...
<br>
<br>

### What Counts as a High-Risk Request?

A request should be treated as high-risk if it asks you to do something that could cause financial loss, data exposure, account compromise, legal risk, or operational disruption.

**Common high-risk requests include:**
<br>
<br>

#### 1. Payment or Banking Requests

Examples:

- “Please process this urgent wire transfer.”
    
- “Our bank details have changed. Use the new account below.”
    
- “This invoice is overdue and must be paid today.”
    
- “Please send payment confirmation once complete.”
    

**Why it's risky:**

Attackers often impersonate executives, suppliers, or finance contacts to redirect payments.

**Correct action:**

Verify through the normal finance approval process and contact the supplier or requester using trusted records, not the contact details in the message.
<br>
<br>

#### 2. Password, MFA, or Login Requests

Examples:

- “Send me the verification code you just received.”
    
- “Approve the MFA prompt so we can complete setup.”
    
- “Your account will be disabled unless you log in here.”
    
- “IT needs your password to fix the issue.”
    

**Why it's risky:**

Legitimate IT teams should not ask for your password or MFA code. Unexpected MFA prompts may mean an attacker already has your password and is trying to get in.

**Correct action:**

Do not share the code, do not approve the prompt, and report it immediately.
<br>
<br>

#### 3. Sensitive Data Requests

**Examples:**

- “Send me the employee salary file.”
    
- “Please export the customer list.”
    
- “Upload the contract documents here.”
    
- “Send over the board materials before the meeting.”
    

**Why it's risky:**

Attackers may use authority, urgency, or confidentiality to pressure employees into exposing valuable information.

**Correct action:**

Confirm the request through an approved internal channel and follow data-sharing rules before sending anything.
<br>
<br>

#### 4. Vendor, Payroll, or Account Changes

**Examples:**

- “Please update our payment account before the next invoice.”
    
- “Change this employee’s direct deposit information.”
    
- “Add this new vendor contact to the system.”
    
- “Update the delivery address for this order.”
    

**Why it's risky:**

These changes can redirect money, goods, or sensitive records to an attacker.

**Correct action:**

Use the official change-control process. Require independent verification before making the change.
<br>
<br>

#### 5. Requests to Bypass Normal Procedures

**Examples:**

- “I know this is unusual, but please handle it just this once.”
    
- “Skip the approval step. We can document it later.”
    
- “Do not involve anyone else yet.”
    
- “This is urgent, so please do not wait for the normal process.”
    

**Why it's risky:**

Attackers often try to isolate the employee from normal controls. A request to bypass process is itself a warning sign.

**Correct action:**

Do not bypass the process. Escalate the request to the appropriate manager or security contact.
<br>
<br>

#### 6. Watch out for the long game

Remain aware that in advanced attacks, the first communications may not include any asks for sensitive data or taking any actions. The attacker may spend a lot of time to build trust and wait to perform their attack much later after rapport has been established.

**Why it's risky:**

By creating trust over an extended period of time, the attacker build trust which can be exploited later.

**Correct action:**

Always remain vigilant, including with connections that are not entirely new but could be false personas that are building up trust gradually to inject an attack one your guard has been let down.

<br>
<br>


### What Is a Separate Channel?

A separate channel is a different, trusted route used to confirm whether the request is legitimate.

**Good separate channels include:**

- A phone number from the company directory
    
- A supplier number from approved vendor records
    
- A known internal chat account
    
- A verified ticketing system
    
- An approved finance, HR, or procurement workflow
    
- A company-approved vendor portal
    
- An in-person confirmation when appropriate
    

**Weak or unsafe verification methods include:**

- Replying to the suspicious email
    
- Calling the phone number inside the message
    
- Clicking the link provided in the message
    
- Scanning the QR code in the message
    
- Using contact details from an attached document
    
- Asking the suspicious sender, “Is this real?”
    
- Continuing the conversation inside the same thread
    

**The key point:**

**Do not let the attacker choose the verification channel.**
<br>
<br>

### The Verification Process

Use this simple process for high-risk requests:
<br>
<br>

#### Step 1: Stop

Do not click, open, approve, send, pay, change, or reply yet.
<br>
<br>

#### Step 2: Identify the Risk

**Ask:**

- Does this involve money?
    
- Does this involve credentials or MFA?
    
- Does this involve sensitive data?
    
- Does this involve a vendor, payroll, or bank-detail change?
    
- Does this ask me to bypass a normal process?
    
- Does this use urgency, secrecy, or pressure?
    

If yes, treat it as high-risk.
<br>
<br>

#### Step 3: Choose a Trusted Channel

Use a contact method or approval system that already existed before the message arrived.

Do not use the details inside the suspicious message.
<br>
<br>

#### Step 4: Verify the Specific Request

Do not ask a vague question like:

- “Did you email me?”

Ask a specific question:

- “Did you request a bank-detail change for invoice 1847?”

- “Did you ask me to send the customer export to this external address?”

- “Did you ask me to approve a login from a new location?”

- “Did you ask for this payment to be processed outside the normal approval workflow?”
<br>
<br>

#### Step 5: Document or Report the Outcome

If the request is legitimate, follow the approved process.

If the request is suspicious, report it using the company’s approved reporting method.

If you already acted on the request, report it immediately.
<br>
<br>

#### Example: Safe Verification

**Suspicious message:**

“Hi, I am in meetings all day. Please process this supplier payment urgently and keep it confidential until I explain later.”

**Unsafe response:**

Replying to the email and asking, “Can you confirm this is real?”

**Safe response:**

Call the executive using the number in the company directory or contact their assistant through an approved internal channel. Confirm the exact payment request before taking action.
<br>
<br>

#### Example: Vendor Bank-Detail Change

**Suspicious message:**

“Please update our banking details before sending the next payment. Our previous account is no longer active.”

**Unsafe response:**

Updating the vendor record based only on the email.

**Safe response:**

Call the vendor using the phone number already stored in the approved vendor record. Confirm the change with a known contact. Follow the company’s vendor-change approval process.
<br>
<br>

#### Example: Unexpected MFA Prompt

**Suspicious event:**

You receive an MFA approval request, but you are not trying to log in.

**Unsafe response:**

Approving the prompt because it might be IT.

**Safe response:**

Deny the prompt and report it immediately. If possible, change your password through the company’s official login route or follow the security team’s instructions.
<br>
<br>

#### Example: Confidential File Request

**Suspicious message:**

“Please send the employee compensation file to this email address. This is for a confidential leadership review.”

**Unsafe response:**

Sending the file because the request appears to come from a senior leader.

**Safe response:**

Verify through an internal trusted channel. Confirm whether the recipient is approved to receive the file. Use the approved secure file-sharing method if the request is legitimate.
<br>
<br>

### Warning Signs That Verification Is Required

**Verify separately when a request includes:**

- Urgency
    
- Secrecy
    
- Pressure from authority
    
- A financial transaction
    
- A new payment method
    
- A new supplier contact
    
- A request for credentials
    
- A request for MFA approval
    
- A request for sensitive data
    
- A request to use personal email
    
- A request to bypass normal steps
    
- A request that feels unusual for the sender
    
- A request involving an external account or link
    

One warning sign may be enough to pause. Multiple warning signs mean the request should be treated as suspicious until verified.
<br>
<br>

### What Employees Should Say

Employees often act because they do not want to appear difficult, slow, or disrespectful. The course should give them safe language they can use.

**Suggested responses:**

- “I need to verify this through our normal process before I can proceed.”

- “For security reasons, I cannot make this change based only on email.”

- “I will confirm this through our approved contact record.”

- “I cannot approve an unexpected login prompt.”

- “I need to route this through the standard approval process.”

- “I am reporting this for review before taking action.”

This language helps employees resist pressure without sounding confrontational.
<br>
<br>

### What Not to Do

**Do not:**

- Reply to the suspicious message to verify it
    
- Use contact details provided in the message
    
- Click a link to confirm the request
    
- Scan a QR code to verify your account
    
- Approve an unexpected MFA prompt
    
- Send passwords, codes, or confidential information
    
- Change bank, payroll, vendor, or delivery details based only on email
    
- Make exceptions because the sender appears senior
    
- Keep the request secret if it breaks normal process
    
- Assume a message is safe because it comes from a familiar account
    
<br>
<br>

### Manager and Executive Assurance Points

Managers and executives should make sure that verification is not left to personal judgment alone. High-risk business actions should require clear procedures.

**Leadership should verify that the company has:**

- Dual approval for significant payments
    
- Independent verification for bank-detail changes
    
- Written procedures for vendor and payroll changes
    
- Clear rules for handling urgent executive requests
    
- A rule that passwords and MFA codes are never shared
    
- A process for verifying sensitive data requests
    
- A process for reviewing changes to supplier, customer, and employee records
    
- Training for finance, HR, procurement, executive assistants, and managers
    
- A simple reporting path for suspicious requests
    
- A culture where employees are allowed to slow down and verify
    
<br>
<br>

### Questions Leaders Should Ask

**Executives and key staff should ask:**

- Can one employee change supplier bank details alone?
    
- Can one employee approve a large payment without independent review?
    
- Are payroll changes independently verified?
    
- Are staff trained to challenge unusual executive requests?
    
- Are vendor records protected from unauthorized changes?
    
- Are approval limits documented and enforced?
    
- Are exceptions logged and reviewed?
    
- Do employees know how to verify requests outside the original message?
    
- Are suspicious requests reported and reviewed for patterns?
    
- Does leadership model the behavior by respecting verification steps?
    


The goal is not to turn executives into IT technicians. The goal is to make sure the company’s processes cannot be easily bypassed by a convincing message.
<br>
<br>


### Key Takeaway

High-risk requests require more than trust. They require verification.

A professional-looking message, familiar name, or urgent tone does not prove that a request is legitimate. Before acting on requests involving money, access, credentials, sensitive data, vendor changes, payroll, or unusual secrecy, verify through a separate trusted channel.

**Do not let the attacker choose the verification method.**
